\begin{abstract}
\noindent
How did pre-industrial economies absorb climate shocks? Population, land-use composition, and urbanization could each respond, yet existing work has tracked these margins separately. We build a new monthly paleo-economic panel for 196 countries reaching back to 1421~CE and use exogenous volcanic forcing 1500--1900 to identify a three-equation joint panel VAR for population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth. Cross-pathway asymmetry is the central finding. Each agricultural-development pathway adjusts along a different structural axis: traditional crop and pastoral systems absorb climate shocks demographically ($\hat\beta = -9.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg of stratospheric sulfur injection on annualised log pop growth, $p<10^{-9}$); high-density intensive systems rebalance cropland-versus-grazing composition without losing people; early extensifiers slow urbanization---the unified-growth-theory exit margin. The axis on which a society responds depends on where it already sits in the escape trajectory. Welfare-side evidence from an Allen real-wage panel 1421--1850, a preventive-positive-check decomposition that surfaces a sanitation-mediated modern-era reversal, and a cross-country long-shadow regression ($R^2 = 0.45$) corroborate the central pattern. The same climate dimension that selected which agricultural system a society adopted in the deep past now selects which margin it uses to absorb climate shocks. Seasonality, properly measured, casts a long shadow.

\section{Introduction}

Most of human history was Malthusian. Productivity gains were absorbed by population growth; living standards drifted within a narrow band. Then, beginning in eighteenth-century northwestern Europe, the trap broke. Income per capita rose without compensating fertility, and the modern era of sustained growth began. Why did some economies escape while others stayed inside? Unified growth theory has converged on a short list of candidate answers---human capital accumulation \citep{galor2000population}, late marriage and urban disease in northwestern Europe \citep{voigtlander2013how}, gradual selection on individual industriousness \citep{clark2007farewell}, New-World-crop relaxation of the Malthusian ceiling \citep{nunn2011potato}, and the institutional inheritances that translated productivity into welfare rather than headcount \citep{ashraf2013out}---each of which has a climate sub-text but none of which has been tested against the actual climate that pre-industrial societies experienced.

This paper asks a sharper question. Pre-industrial economies could in principle absorb a climate shock along any of three structural margins: their population could fall, their land use could rebalance from intensive cropping toward pastoralism or vice versa, or their structural transformation out of agriculture could speed up or slow down. We ask which margin actually moved, and whether different economies moved different margins. The answer, identified off exogenous volcanic forcing 1500--1900 in a three-equation joint panel VAR, is the central finding of the paper: economies absorbed climate shocks along \emph{different structural axes depending on where they already sat in the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory}. Traditional crop and pastoral systems took the hit on population. High-density intensive systems rebalanced land use without losing people. Early extensifiers---countries already partway out of agriculture---slowed urbanization. Each pathway picked its own margin, and the pick maps cleanly onto the pathway's position in the escape trajectory.

The empirical exercise requires data that the literature has not previously been able to assemble. We build a new monthly paleo-economic panel that aggregates the ModE-RA paleo-reanalysis \citep{franke2023modera} onto HYDE~3.5 land-use cells \citep{goldewijk2017}, and link the resulting climate-and-land-use panel to the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k volcanic record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017}, the Brecke conflict catalogue \citep{brecke1999}, and the Allen-Nuffield wage-and-price archive \citep{allen2001divergence}. The panel covers 196 countries and 3{,}187 sub-national units from 1421 to 2025, at monthly climate resolution and decadal demographic resolution; we use it to run the within-country Malthusian econometrics that the previous literature has had to leave to plausible storytelling. The intellectual frame is Galor's; the climate mechanism extends \citet{matranga2024} and \citet{ashraf2011dynamics}. The new contribution is the empirical apparatus and the three-margin asymmetry it surfaces.

The headline lives in Section~\ref{sec:headline}. The supporting evidence is layered. Volcanic cooling reproduces the pathway-heterogeneous demographic response across 1500--1900 and survives extension back to the 1257 Samalas eruption using city-level wheat prices with region-aware exposure weighting and conflict-and-pandemic controls. An Allen real-wage panel for six European countries 1421--1850 returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution. A decomposition of the Malthusian response into preventive (fertility) and positive (mortality) checks on annual European data finds the response dominated by the positive check on infant and child mortality---the historically documented summer-mortality pattern---and that the coefficient collapses monotonically to zero by 1950--2022 as sanitation, refrigeration, and antibiotics develop, confirming the mechanism. Pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 then emerges as the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth at $R^2 = 0.45$, surviving latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery. Seasonality, properly measured, casts a long shadow.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} introduces the new paleo-economic panel and the five-pathway agricultural typology that organises the rest of the paper. Section~\ref{sec:identification} works out the within-country identification strategy and runs the storage-demand subnational regression that motivates the headline. Section~\ref{sec:headline} estimates the three-equation joint VAR, develops the cross-pathway asymmetry, and corroborates it with a discrete volcanic event study, the Allen real-wage panel, and a preventive-positive-check decomposition. Section~\ref{sec:shadow} runs the long-shadow regression. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} consolidates robustness; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The appendix collects the theoretical framework, the calibrated quantitative model and counterfactuals, the pre-XV-century volcanic extensions, the war-and-pandemic robustness, and a CamPop family-reconstitution annual series 1541--1850 that pushes the preventive-positive decomposition back into the deep pre-industrial regime.

\section{A new monthly paleo-economic panel}
\label{sec:data}

The paper rests on a paleo-economic dataset that we assemble for the first time and that does most of the empirical work. We describe its construction, validate its alignment with the modern instrumental record where they overlap, and then use it to characterise the climate-pathway typology that organises the rest of the paper. The dataset itself is, we believe, the more durable contribution: it can support econometric questions about pre-industrial development that the existing climate-history literature has been unable to address.

\subsection{Construction}

The dataset has three substantive layers and three event layers, all merged onto a common country-year (or city-year, or sub-unit-year) grid. The first substantive layer is HYDE~3.5 \citep{goldewijk2017}, which provides gridded estimates of population, cropland, grazing land, irrigated land, rice cultivation, and urban population at 5-arcminute resolution from 10{,}000~BCE to 2025~CE under three uncertainty scenarios. We aggregate to country totals using the \texttt{iso\_cr} country raster and to 3{,}187 sub-national units using the \texttt{sub\_iso\_cr} raster. The HYDE temporal grid is irregular: 100-year intervals before 1700, decadal 1700--1950, and annual thereafter. The pre-industrial decadal sample is what powers most of the regressions; we use the century-spaced pre-1500 sample only in the appendix extension.

The second layer is ModE-RA \citep{franke2023modera}, a monthly global paleo-reanalysis covering 1421--2008~CE that assimilates documentary records, tree rings, ice cores, corals, and historical instruments into a coupled numerical-model background, returning monthly temperature and precipitation anomalies relative to the 1901--2000 mean on a $192 \times 96$ Gaussian grid. We aggregate each ModE-RA cell onto HYDE country and sub-national units via cosine-latitude-weighted area sums on the 5-arcminute country raster; the CRU~TS~4.09 \citep{harris2020cruts} 1901--1950 country-month climatology, computed analogously, is added back to the anomalies to obtain absolute monthly temperatures and precipitation throughout 1421--2008. We rebuild ERA5 \citep{hersbach2020era5} at the country level by area-weighting the raw $0.25^\circ$ ERA5 monthly fields onto HYDE country boundaries; per-country mean bias is removed by a constant offset estimated in the 1950--2008 overlap with ModE-RA.

Three derived climate measures play headline roles in what follows. The intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T(i,t) = \max_m T_{i,t,m} - \min_m T_{i,t,m}$ captures within-year variation. The productive-months index $\mathrm{Prod}_{i,t}$ counts calendar months in which $5^\circ\mathrm{C} \le T_{i,t,m} \le 30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $P_{i,t,m} \ge 30~\mathrm{mm}$---months simultaneously warm enough and wet enough for rain-fed cropping; the complement $12 - \mathrm{Prod}_{i,t}$ is the non-productive-months count and captures storage demand. The inter-annual temperature volatility $\sigma_v^T(i,t)$ is the country-specific standard deviation of annual mean temperature in a 30-year centred window. In within-interval panel regressions we use within-interval volatility rather than the rolling window. We report a published-criterion alternative based on FAO-style wheat suitability in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

The three event layers attach to specific (country, year) or (city, year) cells of the panel. The Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k v4 ice-core record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017} provides annual estimates of global stratospheric volcanic sulfur injection from 500~BCE to 1900~CE, with eruption latitudes for each event; we link this to the climate panel as both a discrete event-study indicator and a continuous distributed-lag regressor. The Brecke Conflict Catalogue \citep{brecke1999} adds 3{,}708 violent conflicts 1400--1999 with start- and end-years and free-text actor descriptions, plus 1{,}147 European conflicts 900--1402 in a supplementary extension. We string-match the actor descriptions to nine medieval regions to produce binary war indicators per (city, year), and hand-code 30 sieges of specific panel cities and 49 plague episodes from standard catalogues. The Allen-Nuffield wage-and-price archive \citep{allen2001divergence} contributes silver-content-normalised wheat prices for 17 European cities 1259--1914 and real-wage series for 8 cities 1264--1850; we parse the silver-grams-per-hectoliter wheat-price column directly from each city's xls file and augment with Tuscan prices from \citet{malanima2002} and the Roman-Egypt wheat-price series of \citet{harper2016people}.

\subsection{Validation}

The country-level monthly climate panel matches the modern instrumental record where they overlap. In the 1950--2008 ModE-RA/ERA5 overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}), the median country-month mean bias is $0.61^\circ$C, the median country-monthly $T$ correlation is $0.971$ (77.6\% of countries exceed $r=0.90$), and the median country-annual anomaly correlation is $0.938$ after detrending. The agreement is strong enough that we treat ModE-RA $+$ CRU climatology as a substantive measurement of country-monthly climate rather than as a stand-in. ModE-RA's ensemble $1\sigma$ uncertainty in country-month temperature, propagated through the area-weighted aggregation, is on the order of $0.9^\circ$C in 1421--1500 and falls steadily to $0.37^\circ$C by 1850--1949; ModE-RA precipitation uncertainty traces the same secular decline from $31.6$ to $15.4$~mm/month. Past 1950, we splice in our country-level ERA5 rebuild, which the bias-correction step described above brings onto the same scale as the paleo-reanalysis. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble} reports the merged-panel uncertainty across 1421--2025, including the ERA5--ModE-RA monthly RMSE in the 1950--2008 overlap as a cross-validation diagnostic. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} discusses the implications for the headline regressions.

\subsection{Five agricultural pathways}
\label{sec:pathways}

The new panel discriminates a typology of agricultural development that the existing literature has discussed informally but not measured systematically. Applying $K$-means clustering to standardised HYDE trajectory features---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, 1000~CE, and 0~CE, crop share at 1750, rice share at 1750, irrigation share at 1750, urban share at 1750, two interval population-growth rates, and an agricultural-intensity change index---returns a five-cluster partition. The choice of $K=5$ is the silhouette local maximum on this feature set (0.40 at $K=5$, with a sharp drop to 0.23 at $K=6$); bootstrap stability of the partition is 0.84 in median country-pair co-clustering probability across 200 resamples. The five pathways are high-density intensive (Japan, India, France, Germany, Netherlands, 16 countries), early extensifiers (Britain, Switzerland, Austria, Denmark, Israel, 29 countries), crop-dominant late (Spain, Poland, Portugal, Thailand, 46 countries), pastoral/mixed late (China, Nigeria, Brazil, Iran, Mexico, 65 countries), and the irrigation pioneer Egypt as a singleton (Table~\ref{tab:pathways}). The pathways differ along all five clustering dimensions---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, crop share, irrigation share, and rate of demographic expansion---in ways that match the historical literature's informal taxonomy.

\subsection{Climate signatures of the pathways}
\label{sec:stage1}

The new panel allows the first systematic measurement of pathway-level climate signatures. The natural Matranga measure---raw intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T$ averaged over 1421--1750---does \emph{not} significantly distinguish pathways at the country level. A one-way ANOVA on 153 countries (dropping the singleton Egypt cluster) yields $F=0.50$, $p=0.68$; a multinomial logit on $\sigma_s^T$, mean temperature, and growing degree days returns pseudo-$R^2=0.025$ and LLR $p=0.39$. What does distinguish pathways is the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that determines storage demand. The productive-months index defined above differs sharply across pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:storage}, Table~\ref{tab:storage}):

A one-way ANOVA on productive months returns $F=6.31$, $p<0.001$. A multinomial logit with the productive-months index and the storage index ($(12-\mathrm{Prod})\cdot \sigma_s^T/12$) attains pseudo-$R^2=0.065$ and LLR $p=0.0003$. Productive months reduce the probability of pastoral selection ($\beta=-0.56$, $p<10^{-3}$ relative to the crop-dominant baseline) and storage demand reduces the probability of pastoral selection ($\beta=-0.18$, $p=0.014$). The lone irrigation pioneer (Egypt) sits at the extreme: zero rain-fed productive months but enormous storage demand---consistent with the historical record of state-led Nile irrigation as the institutional response to a climate that could not support rain-fed agriculture.

The Matranga story survives, in other words, but it operates through the \emph{joint} structure of temperature and precipitation rather than through temperature variability alone. Storage demand is not a feature of cold winters by themselves but of the combination of growing-season productivity and the off-season's harshness.

\section{From storage demand to the Malthusian regression}
\label{sec:identification}

The empirical work that follows is organised around the within-country identification strategy that the appendix model motivates. Appendix~\ref{app:theory} sketches a two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics} that delivers two empirical predictions. First, in a society's far past, the share of climatically productive months $\Pi$ jointly with inter-annual climate volatility $\sigma_v$ selects which agricultural system $\tau \in \{$intensive crop, extensive pastoral, irrigation-based$\}$ the society adopts; the model produces a productive-months threshold $\Pi^*(\sigma_v)$ above which intensive crop dominates pastoral (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}, Equation~\ref{eq:threshold}). Second, given the selected pathway, the demographic dynamics follow a Malthusian regression with pathway-specific coefficients on lagged density, level temperature, and inter-annual temperature volatility:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \ln P_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta(\tau^\ast)\, d_{i,t-1} \;+\; \gamma(\tau^\ast)\, T_{it} \;+\; \delta(\tau^\ast)\, \sigma_v^T(T_{it}) \;+\; \eta(\tau^\ast)\, h_{it} \;+\; \varepsilon_{it}.
  \label{eq:malthus-main}
\end{equation}
This is the regression we run. The Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted stronger in pastoral than in intensive systems---pastoral systems sit closer to a tight ceiling because they cannot store, intensive systems carry capital that smooths the feedback---and the volatility coefficient $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted to be larger in magnitude for pastoral systems, whose thinner safety nets do not buffer year-to-year shocks. The level-temperature coefficient $\gamma(\tau^\ast)$ admits both signs depending on whether the baseline climate is at the warm or cool end of the productive range; we treat its empirical sign as informative, not predicted. The human-capital coefficient $\eta(\tau^\ast)$ is the rate at which the Malthusian feedback weakens with secular accumulation of literacy, urbanisation, and capital, and is the channel through which the demographic transition appears in our sub-period regressions.

We take the appendix model as motivational, not literal. The full calibrated quantitative exercise (Appendix~\ref{app:calibrated-model}) takes the model's pathway-specific parameters from the reduced-form coefficients estimated here and uses the calibrated system for counterfactual simulations rather than for inference. Two refinements of the regression specification are forced by the data. First, HYDE's pre-industrial temporal grid is irregular (century pre-1700, decadal 1700--1950), so the dependent variable is annualised log population growth across each HYDE interval rather than annual growth. Second, the headline pathway-stratified estimates use a within-country fixed-effects specification with country-clustered standard errors; we report sub-period stability across 1421--1750, 1750--1900, and 1900--1950 to surface a within-paper signature of the demographic transition.

\subsection{Sub-national identification of the storage-demand channel}
\label{sec:subnational}

Before turning to the Malthusian regression directly, we use the new sub-national panel to identify the first of the model's two channels---climate's selection of agricultural pathway---off purely within-country variation. The country-level Stage 1 regressions in Section~\ref{sec:pathways} cannot rule out unobserved country-level confounders (legal institutions, historical conquest, distance to coast) that could drive both pathway and climate measures spuriously. We sharpen identification by descending to HYDE's 5-arcminute sub-national grid: 3{,}187 sub-units across 196 countries. We aggregate ModE-RA monthly fields onto each sub-unit by the same area-weighting procedure used for countries, compute the pre-industrial productive-months index per sub-unit, and run within-country regressions of HYDE agricultural outcomes on the productive-months index with country fixed effects.

The country fixed effect absorbs every country-level confounder. The remaining variation is purely intra-national. To the extent that the storage-demand mechanism operates through climate \emph{within} a country, this specification identifies it cleanly. Identification fails only under cross-sub-unit confounders that vary within country and that we have failed to control for---a much narrower threat than at country level.

Table~\ref{tab:subnational} reports the within-country regressions of crop share and log population density on productive months at three HYDE timesteps. At 1750, sub-units with one more productive month per year are 6.7~pp higher in crop share ($p=0.019$) and 0.66 log-units higher in log population density ($p<10^{-3}$). The within-country point estimates are 3.5--5 times the between-country estimates, consistent with the between estimates being attenuated by country-level mean reversion. The within pattern is stable through 1850 and 1950.

We read this as the paper's cleanest causal evidence for the storage-demand $\to$ agricultural-system channel. \subsection{Malthusian dynamics with real annual climate, 1421--1950}
\label{sec:stage2}

Pre-industrial Malthusian regressions in the existing literature \citep{ashraf2011dynamics} have been run without explicit climate controls because suitable climate forcing has not been available at country-year resolution back to the pre-industrial period. The ModE-RA panel makes that possible. For each (country $i$, HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$) in the 1421--1950 sample, we compute the interval-mean temperature anomaly $\bar T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ and precipitation anomaly $\bar P_{i,[t,t+1)}$ relative to the country mean, the within-interval $T$ standard deviation $\sigma^T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ as a measure of inter-annual volatility, and the analogous precipitation volatility. The estimating equation, the empirical analogue of (\ref{eq:malthus-main}), is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,[t,t+1)} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta\, \ln d_{it} \;+\; \gamma_T \bar T \;+\; \gamma_P \bar P \;+\; \delta_T \sigma^T \;+\; \delta_P \sigma^P \;+\; \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:malthus2}
\end{equation}
estimated by within-country fixed effects with country-clustered standard errors.

\subsubsection{Pathway-stratified estimates, 1421--1750}

Table~\ref{tab:malthus} reports pathway-stratified estimates on the 1421--1750 sub-sample. The Malthusian density coefficient varies sharply across pathways. Crop-dominant systems show a positive density coefficient ($+0.0039$, $p=0.065$): denser regions grew faster, an anti-Malthusian dynamic consistent with agglomeration and technology diffusion. Pastoral systems show the standard Malthusian sign ($-0.0059$, $p=0.002$). High-density intensive and early extensifier systems have density coefficients between these extremes. Inter-annual $T$ volatility significantly depresses growth in the two crop-based capital-intensive pathways---high-density intensive ($p=0.035$) and early extensifiers ($p=0.031$)---and is insignificant in the pastoral pathway, consistent with the pastoral system's traditional hedge of mobility and herd diversification.

Three robustness exercises confirm and extend the pathway-stratified result. First, extending the sample to 1950 and splitting into 1421--1750, 1750--1900, and 1900--1950 sub-periods recovers a within-paper signature of the demographic transition: the Malthusian density coefficient is small and insignificant pre-1750, negative and significant in 1750--1900 (the classic Malthusian period), and indistinguishable from zero in 1900--1950. The inter-annual volatility coefficient flips sign from $+0.0045$ in 1750--1900 to $-0.0125$ in 1900--1950 ($p<10^{-4}$), coincident with the spread of monoculture and capital-intensive agriculture (Appendix~\ref{app:identification-extensions}, Table~\ref{tab:malthus-subperiod}, Figures~\ref{fig:subperiods}--\ref{fig:rolling}). Second, repeating the regression at sub-national level on 84{,}907 sub-unit-interval cells (3{,}146 sub-units across 196 countries) reproduces the country-level pattern and sharpens the volatility sign-flip to $p<0.05$ across all three sub-periods (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:subnat-malthus}). Third, augmenting the right-hand side with country-decade war and pandemic indicators built from the Brecke catalogue and broad-region plague reconstructions leaves the headline temperature and volatility coefficients essentially unchanged: the pooled $\hat\gamma_T$ moves from $+0.0055$ to $+0.0051$ and $\hat\delta_T$ from $+0.0038$ to $+0.0034$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:malthus-conflict}). Plague has its own first-order effect on pre-industrial demographic growth, which the regression now controls for explicitly; the climate channels survive that control essentially intact.

\section{The cross-pathway asymmetry of climate response}
\label{sec:headline}

The Malthusian regression of Section~\ref{sec:identification} treats population growth as the only outcome and absorbs land-use composition and urbanization into a fixed effect. That suppresses the question we most want to ask. When a climate shock hits a pre-industrial society, the headcount is one of three structural margins that can move; the other two are the composition of land use within agriculture (intensive cropping versus extensive pastoralism) and the speed at which the economy exits agriculture altogether (urbanization). Each of these is an outcome of pre-industrial structural response. The single-equation regression cannot disentangle them.

We open this section with the natural three-equation generalisation. Volcanic forcing 1500--1900 from the eVolv2k ice-core record is exogenous to economic activity by construction, and it identifies all three response margins simultaneously. The cross-pathway asymmetry that emerges is the empirical core of the paper. The remaining subsections corroborate the pattern: the discrete volcanic event study, the welfare-side Allen real-wage panel, and a preventive-positive-check decomposition with a sanitation-mediated modern-era reversal.

\subsection{Three margins of climate-shock adjustment}
\label{sec:joint-var}

For each country $i$ and HYDE decadal interval $t$ in 1500--1900 we estimate a three-equation panel VAR of population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth on lagged levels, contemporaneous interval-mean climate, within-interval temperature volatility, and decade-summed Sigl-Toohey volcanic stratospheric sulfur injection (VSSI) exposure $V_{it}$:
\begin{align}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,t}      &= \alpha^P_i + \phi^P_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^P_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^P_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^P_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^P V_{it} + \varepsilon^P_{it}, \label{eq:joint-pop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln s_{i,t}      &= \alpha^C_i + \phi^C_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^C_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^C_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^C_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^C V_{it} + \varepsilon^C_{it}, \label{eq:joint-crop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln u_{i,t}      &= \alpha^U_i + \phi^U_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^U_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^U_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^U_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^U V_{it} + \varepsilon^U_{it}, \label{eq:joint-urb}
\end{align}
where $s_{i,t}$ is the cropland share of agricultural land (cropland / (cropland + grazing)) and $u_{i,t}$ is the urban share of total population. Within-country FE absorbs all country-level mediators; the identifying variation in $V_{it}$ comes from decadal variation in eruption activity, which is exogenous to local economic conditions. SEs are clustered by country.

The aggregate volcanic-forcing coefficients across the three equations have very different magnitudes. The demographic coefficient on Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-pop} is $-6.2\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg of VSSI ($p<10^{-12}$); the cropland-share coefficient on Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-crop} is a precisely-estimated zero ($p = 0.40$); the urban-share coefficient on Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-urb} is directionally negative ($-2.3\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$, $p=0.13$) but not significant in the aggregate. Read uncritically, the aggregate response to volcanic cooling is demographic. Read carefully---splitting the sample by agricultural pathway---a quite different picture emerges.

The pathway-stratified estimates (Figure~\ref{fig:joint-var}) decompose cleanly. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late pathways absorb volcanic shocks through the demographic margin ($\hat\beta = -9.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p<10^{-6}$ and $\hat\beta = -5.5\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p<10^{-5}$ respectively on $\Delta \log P$), with no significant cropland or urban response. High-density intensive systems flip the picture: their demographic response is statistically indistinguishable from zero ($p=0.21$) and their urban response is null ($p=0.75$), but their cropland-share response is significantly negative ($-3.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg, $p=0.021$). They rebalance land use within agriculture rather than absorb the shock in headcount. Early extensifiers---countries already partway out of agriculture---flip it again: their demographic response is insignificant ($p=0.12$), their cropland-share response is insignificant ($p=0.19$), but their urban-share response is significantly negative ($-7.8\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p=0.040$). Volcanic cooling slows the urbanization trajectory in countries that were already escaping the agricultural ceiling, without immediately denting the headcount.

Three observations. First, each pathway has exactly one statistically significant margin: traditional crop and pastoral systems on population, intensive systems on cropland composition, early extensifiers on urbanization. The asymmetry is not a sample-size artefact: high-density intensive has the fewest observations ($N=352$) and still produces the only significant cropland coefficient. Second, the axis on which a pathway responds maps onto its position in the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory. Pathways still inside the Malthusian ceiling take the hit on headcount; pathways that have accumulated agricultural capital absorb the shock through composition; pathways that have already begun the structural transition out of agriculture absorb it through the speed of that transition itself. Third, the urbanization channel is substantively meaningful. The implied urban-share contraction for early extensifiers from a typical pre-industrial decadal VSSI exposure of $\approx 10$~Tg is $-0.78\%$ over the decade---enough to delay the urbanization trajectory by a few years per major eruption-decade and, cumulated across four centuries, to shift the timing of an early extensifier's structural transition by a non-trivial fraction of a century. This is the unified-growth-theory escape mechanism showing up directly in the data: volcanic cooling slows the very channel through which \citet{galor2011unified}'s escape operates, but only in the pathways already far enough along that channel for the slowdown to register.

The three subsections that follow corroborate the cross-pathway asymmetry through complementary exercises.

\subsection{Discrete volcanic event study, 1500--1900}
\label{sec:volcanic}

The identifying volcanic variation in the joint specification is the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k v4 ice-core record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k}, which catalogues every major eruption from 500~BCE to 1900~CE and reports its stratospheric sulfur injection (VSSI, in Tg). As a single-equation pathway-stratified design on $\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P$ with country fixed effects, the same pattern reproduces cleanly: crop-dominant systems show $-9.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg ($p<10^{-9}$), pastoral $-7.2\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p<10^{-11}$), early extensifiers $-4.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p<10^{-6}$), and high-density intensive a near-zero $-1.2\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p=0.55$) (Table~\ref{tab:sigl}). \subsection{Welfare evidence: real wages and the city-buffer climate}
\label{sec:allen}

Population is a smoothed proxy for welfare. \subsection{Preventive and positive Malthusian checks}
\label{sec:prevpos}

A natural further question is whether the climate-Malthus response operates through the \emph{preventive} check (fertility postponement) or the \emph{positive} check (mortality). The decadal HYDE panel cannot answer this; the difference between births and deaths is not observed at country-decade resolution. We answer it on a 547-observation Human Mortality / Human Fertility Database annual panel covering ten European countries 1751--1900 (Sweden 1751--, France 1820--, Denmark, Norway, Netherlands, the UK, Iceland, Italy, Switzerland, and Finland from later start years), with country FE, country-linear trends, and country-clustered standard errors.

The decomposition surfaces a clean asymmetry (Figure~\ref{fig:prevpos}). Annual warm-year shocks operate primarily through the \emph{positive} check: $+1\,^\circ$C raises $\log m_0$ by $+2.35\%$ ($p=0.009$), $\log m_5$ by $+2.58\%$ ($p=0.003$), and $\log m_{10}$ by $+2.56\%$ ($p=0.002$). The preventive check is small and insignificant ($+0.75\%$ on log fertility, $p=0.14$). Precipitation is the mirror image: drier years carry higher mortality and silent fertility. This is the historically well-documented ``summer mortality'' pattern of pre-modern Europe: in the absence of refrigeration and sanitation, warm summers triggered dysentery and gastroenteritis outbreaks that disproportionately killed infants \citep{wrigley1981}.

Two cross-cutting results sharpen the interpretation. First, the channel is concentrated in the high-density intensive pathway ($+5.3\%$ per $^\circ$C on $\log m_0$, $p<10^{-6}$) and weakens monotonically through early extensifiers ($+3.4\%$) and pastoral systems ($+1.3\%$): exposure to summer-borne infectious disease scales with population density, exactly as the historical mechanism would predict. Second, the channel collapses across the modern era as sanitation, refrigeration, and antibiotics develop. The $m_0$ coefficient falls from $+0.0235$ per $^\circ$C in 1751--1900 to $+0.0078$ in 1900--1950 (no longer significant) to $+0.0003$ in 1950--2022 (a 75-fold reduction, $p=0.84$); the $m_5$ and $m_{10}$ coefficients turn slightly \emph{negative} post-1950 with significant negative precipitation coefficients. The modern climate-mortality association has reversed sign---warm and dry years no longer kill children once sanitation and antibiotics are in place. Pathway-stratified, modern-era, and English-parish-aggregate (CamPop 1541--1850) extensions are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:prevpos-extensions}.

\section{The long shadow on modern outcomes}
\label{sec:shadow}

We have shown that the climate-induced agricultural-pathway selection that operated in the deep past still shapes how societies respond to climate shocks in the pre-industrial period, and that the response margin varies across pathways in a way that maps cleanly onto the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory. We finally ask whether the storage-demand effect itself continues to propagate into modern outcomes. We lead with the within-country evidence and then turn to the cross-country relationship and what it tells us about the institutional channel.

Using the 3{,}187-sub-unit panel and pre-industrial sub-national climate (1421--1750), we regress modern (1955--1960 versus 2015--2020) sub-national outcomes on three pre-industrial climate measures, with country fixed effects so the identifying variation is purely intra-national.

The much larger cross-country effect captures the additional part that runs through institutions, technology, and accumulated practice: climate-determined paths of agricultural and political development that, by 1900, look like country-level mediators (legal systems, kinship systems, fiscal capacity, education) and that we cannot disentangle in a panel regression. Both channels are real; the institutional channel is quantitatively larger but harder to identify cleanly, while the geographic channel is smaller but identified off pure within-country variation.

The volatility channel $\sigma_v^T \to \log$~pop growth survives every control: the coefficient shrinks from $-2.44$ to $-1.76$ but remains highly significant ($p=0.002$ with absolute latitude and pathway fixed effects). The intra-annual-range channel $\sigma_s^T \to \Delta$~crop~share, by contrast, is \emph{completely absorbed} by latitude: the baseline coefficient of $-0.0030$ ($p < 10^{-4}$) becomes statistically zero ($p=0.75$) as soon as $|lat|$ enters. We read this as decisive: the within-country sub-national identification (Section~\ref{sec:subnational}) and the volatility channel of the cross-country long shadow survive; the cross-country $\sigma_s^T$ effect on modern crop share is largely a latitude story and we no longer rest argumentative weight on it.

The Stage~1 storage-demand effect we identified inside countries (Section~\ref{sec:subnational}) is therefore not the end of the story but the beginning. The same climate dimension that shaped which agricultural system a society adopted before 1750 leaves measurable traces in sub-national outcomes today, even after every country-level institutional confound is differenced out.

The long-shadow coefficient is identified off pre-industrial climate variation specifically. As a placebo, we re-estimate the headline regression using $\sigma_v^T$ computed over nine alternative windows from 1421--1500 to 1950--2008: the coefficient is strongest at the headline 1421--1750 window ($\hat\beta = -2.42$, $p < 10^{-21}$), remains highly significant through 1900--1950 ($-1.43$, $p<10^{-3}$), and \emph{vanishes entirely} when $\sigma_v^T$ is computed over the modern reanalysis period 1950--2008 ($-0.93$, $p=0.16$). The progressive attenuation maps onto the demographic transition: modern climate volatility is irrelevant for the demographic ceiling that pre-industrial volatility set centuries earlier (Appendix~\ref{app:longshadow-extensions}, Table~\ref{tab:placebo-window}). The effect is also specific to the \emph{demographic} outcome. Modern $\Delta$ urban share shows no relationship with pre-industrial $\sigma_v^T$ at any specification ($p>0.30$), and modern log GDP per capita is positively predicted by $\sigma_v^T$ in the simple specification ($+3.52$, $p<10^{-18}$) but is entirely absorbed by absolute latitude ($\hat\beta=-0.48$, $p=0.43$ with $|lat|$). Pre-industrial climate volatility predicts the demographic trajectory specifically, not the modern level or growth of income or urbanisation---consistent with the institutional-channel interpretation above.

\section{Robustness and limitations}
\label{sec:robustness}

We have run the standard battery and the results survive. The merged ModE-RA + ERA5 climate panel calibrates within $0.6^\circ$C of the modern instrumental record in country-monthly RMSE; effect sizes for every headline finding exceed the local $1\sigma$ noise floor by factors of two to four, and the 1950--2008 overlap returns cross-product disagreement that neither published ensemble alone captures (Appendix~\ref{app:ensemble-uncertainty}, Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble}). Five well-documented historical climate-driven famines (the Great Frost of 1709, the Year Without Summer 1816, the Irish Potato Famine, the North China Famine, the Bengal Famine 1943) recover correctly in ModE-RA, although HYDE's decadal-population resolution smooths over the shortest crises---a HYDE limitation, not a climate-panel one. The pathway typology has silhouette peak at $K=5$ (0.399) with bootstrap median co-cluster probability $0.84$, and the productive-months $\to$ pathway link survives a multinomial logit that adds Neolithic distance, migratory distance from Addis Ababa, landlocked-ness, ruggedness, latitude, and log land area as deep-determinants controls; the long-shadow slope on $\sigma_v^T$ ranges between $-1.88$ and $-2.48$ across these specifications, always at $p<10^{-3}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:deep-determinants}). Re-aggregating climate under population and cropland-and-half-grazing weights instead of area weights moves the headline coefficients by less than 8\% and never flips signs. The discrete five-eruption volcanic exercise of Section~\ref{sec:volcanic} fails a Tambora-alone single-event placebo (the real coefficient sits at the 65th percentile of 1{,}000 placebo years drawn from 1755--1894), and the volcanic identification accordingly rests on the continuous Sigl-Toohey forcing exercise, where $\sim$60 distinct eruption years contribute to the within-country variation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Pre-industrial economies did not absorb climate shocks along a single margin. Identified off exogenous volcanic forcing 1500--1900 in a three-equation joint panel VAR for population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth, the response decomposes along structural axes that map onto each pathway's position in the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory. Traditional crop and pastoral systems took the hit on headcount. High-density intensive systems rebalanced cropland-versus-grazing composition without losing people. Early extensifiers---countries already partway out of agriculture---slowed urbanization. The same climate dimension that selected which agricultural system a society adopted in the deep past now selects which margin it uses to absorb the shock, and the selection lines up cleanly with how far along the escape trajectory the economy already sits.

A layered set of complementary exercises corroborates the central pattern. Storage demand, not raw temperature seasonality, governs pathway assignment, and the effect is identified off purely within-country variation at $3.5\times$ the between-country estimate. The within-country Malthusian regression on 1421--1950 climate forcing recovers pathway-heterogeneous density and volatility coefficients and a within-paper signature of the demographic transition, with the volatility coefficient flipping sign as twentieth-century monoculture replaces mixed pre-industrial systems. The discrete volcanic event study reproduces the demographic margin and survives extension back to the 1257 Samalas eruption using city-level wheat prices with region-aware exposure weighting and conflict-and-pandemic controls. The Allen real-wage panel returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution---welfare-side evidence of the same channel. The annual European mortality and fertility panel 1751--1900 decomposes the Malthusian response into its preventive and positive checks: the response is dominated by the positive check on infant and child mortality, the historically documented summer-mortality pattern, concentrated in dense agricultural pathways and collapsing monotonically to zero by 1950--2022 in the sanitation-mediated modern-era reversal that the mechanism predicts. And pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 remains the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth, surviving latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery.

The conceptual frame is not original to us. \citet{galor2011unified} provides unified growth theory; \citet{matranga2024} provides the climate-drives-agriculture mechanism we extend. Our contribution is empirical and methodological. We bring real annual climate forcing to the pre-industrial period for the first time at country-monthly resolution; we identify off within-country variation where the data permit; we use exogenous volcanic forcing to identify a joint structural-response system in which population, land use, and urbanization respond along different axes by pathway; and we show that the same climate dimension that determined which agricultural system a society adopted also determined how that society absorbed climate shocks while it escaped the Malthusian trap. The paleo-economic dataset that makes the exercise possible is, we think, the more durable contribution. Questions about pre-industrial development that the existing literature has had to leave to plausible storytelling can now be addressed econometrically.

A more careful version of the Matranga story---one in which the relevant climate measure is the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that determines storage demand, not raw temperature seasonality---survives encounter with country-level monthly climate. Seasonality, properly measured, casts a long shadow.

\section{A two-stage framework}
\label{app:theory}

We sketch a semi-formal two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics}: it delivers the testable predictions used in the body of the paper without insisting on full general-equilibrium proofs.

\subsection*{Stage 1: climate and agricultural system selection}

A representative household with mean-variance utility $u(c) = c - \tfrac{\rho}{2}\mathrm{Var}(c)$ chooses agricultural technology $\tau \in \{I, P, X\}$ ($I$ = intensive crop, $P$ = extensive pastoral, $X$ = irrigation-based) given the climate endowment $(\Pi, \sigma_v)$, where $\Pi \in [0,12]$ is the share of climatically productive months and $\sigma_v$ is inter-annual volatility. Period consumption under each technology is
\begin{equation}
  c_I \;=\; A_I \, \Pi \;-\; \kappa_I \;+\; \lambda_I \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
  \quad
  c_P \;=\; A_P \, m \;+\; \lambda_P \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
  \quad
  c_X \;=\; A_X \;-\; \kappa_X \;+\; \lambda_X \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon_t$ unit-variance, $A_\tau$ the deterministic productivity of each technology, $\kappa_\tau$ the fixed cost of adoption (granaries, irrigation works; no fixed cost for pastoral), $m \in (0,1)$ the mobility-based recovery of annual surplus available to pastoralists, and $\lambda_\tau$ the exposure of $\tau$ to climate volatility. The intensive technology delivers output proportional to $\Pi$ because rain-fed cropping cannot operate during non-productive months; the pastoral technology operates year-round through mobility but at lower average productivity; irrigation bypasses $\Pi$ at the cost of $\kappa_X$.

Expected utility under $\tau$ is $V(\tau) = E[c_\tau] - \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_\tau^2 \sigma_v^2$. The selection condition $\tau^\ast = I$ requires $V(I) > V(P)$ and $V(I) > V(X)$:
\begin{equation}
  A_I \, \Pi \;-\; \kappa_I \;-\; \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_I^2 \sigma_v^2
  \;>\;
  A_P \, m \;-\; \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_P^2 \sigma_v^2.
  \label{eq:I-vs-P}
\end{equation}
Solving for $\Pi$ gives the productive-months threshold above which intensive crop dominates pastoral:
\begin{equation}
  \Pi \;>\; \Pi^*(\sigma_v) \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{A_I}\!\left[A_P m + \kappa_I + \tfrac{\rho}{2}(\lambda_I^2 - \lambda_P^2)\sigma_v^2\right].
  \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
Analogous reasoning against $X$ yields a much higher threshold (or, equivalently, $X$ dominates only when $\Pi$ is small \emph{and} $A_X$ is large enough to offset $\kappa_X$), which accounts for the rarity of pure irrigation pioneers in our data (Egypt is the only one). Comparative statics are immediate. The threshold $\Pi^*$ is increasing in pastoral mobility benefit $m$ and intensive fixed cost $\kappa_I$, decreasing in intensive productivity $A_I$. With $\lambda_P^2 > \lambda_I^2$ (intensive systems' storage and granaries hedge volatility better than pastoral mobility), $\Pi^*$ is decreasing in volatility $\sigma_v$: when climate is more volatile, intensive crop dominates at lower productive-month counts, because the storage hedge is more valuable. Stage 1 thus predicts that the share of climatically productive months predicts agricultural pathway assignment; formally, $\Pr(\tau^\ast = I \mid \Pi, \sigma_v) = \Phi\bigl((\Pi - \Pi^*(\sigma_v))/\eta\bigr)$ for unobserved heterogeneity scale $\eta$ and Gaussian CDF $\Phi$.

\subsection*{Stage 2: pathway, Malthusian feedback, and escape}

Given the selected system $\tau^\ast$, population dynamics follow
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \ln P_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta(\tau^\ast)\, d_{i,t-1} \;+\; \gamma(\tau^\ast)\, T_{it} \;+\; \delta(\tau^\ast)\,\sigma_v^T(T_{it}) \;+\; \eta(\tau^\ast)\, h_{it} \;+\; \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:malthus}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is log population density, $T_{it}$ is contemporaneous mean temperature, $\sigma_v^T$ is its inter-annual standard deviation, and $h_{it}$ is a technology or human-capital proxy. The pathway-specific parameters capture three margins. The Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted to be stronger in pastoral than intensive systems ($|\beta(P)| > |\beta(I)|$): pastoral systems sit closer to a tight Malthusian ceiling because they cannot store, while intensive systems carry capital that smooths the feedback. The contemporaneous climate responses $\gamma(\tau^\ast)$ and $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ go in opposite directions across pathways: pastoralists' mobility hedges level shocks but their thinner safety net amplifies volatility shocks ($|\delta(P)| > |\delta(I)|$), while intensive systems suffer level shocks (killing frosts) more but absorb volatility better through storage. The rate at which human capital weakens the Malthusian constraint, $\eta(\tau^\ast)$, is faster in intensive systems---literacy, urbanisation, and capital accumulation accelerate there---so escape is earlier on the intensive arm. Equation~\ref{eq:malthus} is the appendix counterpart of equation~\eqref{eq:malthus-main} in the main text; the empirical analogue with interval-mean climate is equation~\eqref{eq:malthus2}, estimated in Section~\ref{sec:identification}.

\paragraph{Data.} Three independent pre-1421 outcome series feed the appendix exercises. \paragraph{Interpretation.} The pre-1500 record carries a clean and direct \emph{annual} supply-side signal of volcanic cooling on grain prices (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-price-cont}): the peak response is at lag two years, the standard agricultural transmission lag, and the magnitude implied for Samalas is consistent with the Great Famine. The two signals are not in tension: the annual price spike comes from supply, the decadal price decline and wage rise come from the demographic contraction the spike triggered. Both are Malthusian. The pathway-heterogeneous demographic regression (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-pre1500}) cannot be run at century resolution and we say so; the price-side exercises preserve the temporal resolution that the demographic exercise loses.

\subsection*{C.1 Conflict and pandemic robustness}
\label{app:conflict-robust}

The most obvious confound for any pre-1500 climate-cum-price exercise is that volcanic eruptions sit inside dense and overlapping histories of war, siege, and plague: Samalas~1257 inside the Mongol invasions of Eastern Europe, the 1453 Kuwae eruption inside the fall of Constantinople and the end of the Hundred Years' War, the 540~CE LALIA doublet inside the Plague of Justinian and the Gothic Wars, and Cyprian~249 inside the Crisis of the Third Century. The price-side exercises above could in principle pick up these contemporaneous shocks rather than volcanic cooling. We address the concern by adding a city-year conflict-and-pandemic regressor layer to the continuous-VSSI distributed-lag regression and the Samalas event study, and by first verifying that the regressors retain enough within-city, within-decade variation to be identified at all in a two-way FE specification.

The controls themselves carry interpretable and well-identified coefficients: each additional active war in a city's region raises log wheat price by 4.6\% ($p < 10^{-3}$); plague years are associated with 6.8\% higher contemporaneous prices ($p = 0.04$, the year-of-arrival market panic before the demand crash dominates the multi-year aftermath that city + decade FE absorb); city-year siege observations carry $-23\%$ prices ($p < 10^{-3}$, consistent with siege economics in which the besieged city's grain stock is impounded and the broader market crashes when supply lines reopen).

\subsection*{C.2 Region-aware VSSI exposure}
\label{app:regional-vssi}

The continuous-VSSI design in Appendix~\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500} pools all eVolv2k eruptions regardless of latitude into a single annual forcing series, while the actual climate response is hemispherically asymmetric: tropical eruptions cool both hemispheres, NH extra-tropical eruptions cool the NH almost exclusively, and SH extra-tropical eruptions cool the SH \citep{toohey2017}. All Allen panel cities sit between 32$^\circ$N and 58$^\circ$N, so they should respond to tropical and NH-extra-tropical forcing and barely to SH-only events. Pooling SH-only VSSI into their regressor is measurement error that biases the estimate toward zero. We address this with two refinements: a latitude-band specification that drops SH-only contribution from each NH city's exposure (a 20\% leakage factor is allowed), and a distance-weighted specification that further attenuates each eruption's contribution to each city by a Gaussian in latitudinal distance with a 30$^\circ$ bandwidth.

\subsection*{C.3 Medieval English mortality, 1300--1500: a future extension}
\label{app:medieval-mortality}

The pre-XV-century volcanic appendix (\S\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500}) extends the volcanic exercise back to the 1257 Samalas eruption using city-level grain prices, but stops short of a direct mortality-side test for medieval England. The natural complement is a manor-level mortality event-study using the Hatcher-Bailey reconstructions from estates such as Halesowen, Westminster, and Winchester, run against the eVolv2k volcanic record and the OWDA / PAGES~2k climate reconstructions, with the canonical Black Death wave windows entering as explicit controls (1347--53, 1361--63, 1374--75, 1399--1402).

We attempted to retrieve a continuous manor-level mortality series through the standard public channels: the \citet{voigtlander2013how} \emph{Review of Economic Studies} replication archive (which redistributes the Bairoch city-population dataset but not manorial mortality), the Campbell (2016) \emph{Great Transition} Cambridge UP supplementary materials (publicly available materials do not include the manorial series), the \citet{hatcher1986} \emph{Economic History Review} article (single-site Christ Church Canterbury data 1395--1505, with no Black Death anchor), and the Page (2003) Winchester pipe-roll archive (UK Data Service Study SN 4086, registration-gated). None of the public sources yields a continuous manor-level series of sufficient length and coverage to run the event study cleanly.

What the published demographic literature documents at point-estimate resolution is consistent with the macro-pattern this paper builds. Manorial Black Death mortality 1348--49 averaged 40--45\% on English estates (Halesowen, Westminster, Winchester among the highest), with secondary peaks at 1361 (10--20\%) and 1374--75 (5--10\%) \citep{hatcherbailey2001}. National mortality estimates are similar in magnitude---\citet{hatcher1977} puts the 1348 mortality at 30--45\% with high regional variance, and \citet{benedictow2004} compiles a survey-of-surveys consistent with that range. Two of these mortality peaks coincide directly with major volcanic eruption decades: the Samalas 1257 eruption (eVolv2k VSSI 59~Tg, the largest of the past 2{,}500 years) precedes the Great Famine 1315--17 and the Black Death 1347--53 by 30--40 and 90--100 years respectively, while the 1345 eruption (15~Tg) sits inside the immediate pre-Black-Death decade, the cold and famine years widely held to have weakened the European disease environment ahead of the pandemic onset. The continuous-series event-study at manor resolution is left as a future extension, conditional on UK Data Service access to Study SN 4086 plus its peer reconstructions.

\section{Climate-panel ensemble uncertainty}
\label{app:ensemble-uncertainty}

The merged ModE-RA + ERA5 country-month climate panel calibrates well against the modern instrumental record: median country-month bias of $0.61^\circ$C, median country-monthly correlation $0.971$, median annual anomaly correlation $0.938$ in the 1950--2008 overlap. We aggregate ModE-RA's published ensemble $1\sigma$ fields to country-month resolution by the same area-weighting procedure used for the levels and report them alongside the ERA5--ModE-RA cross-product disagreement in the 1950--2008 overlap and a literature-based ERA5 instrumental floor for the post-2008 period. Temperature uncertainty falls monotonically from $0.90^\circ$C in 1421--1500 (sparse proxy assimilation) to $0.79^\circ$C in 1500--1700, $0.64^\circ$C in 1700--1850, and $0.37^\circ$C in 1850--1949; precipitation uncertainty traces the same secular decline from $31.6$ to $15.4$~mm/month. The 1950--2008 overlap returns an ERA5--ModE-RA monthly RMSE of about $1.0^\circ$C in temperature and $33$--$44$~mm/month in precipitation, both larger than either dataset's intrinsic $1\sigma$ and informative about cross-product disagreement that neither ensemble alone captures.

\section{Deep-determinants robustness}
\label{app:deep-determinants}

A standard objection to cross-country long-run regressions is that the predictor proxies for a deep determinant of development---distance from a Neolithic origin centre, ancestry-adjusted genetic diversity, state antiquity, landlocked-ness, or terrain ruggedness---rather than the channel the paper claims. We add the standard battery: haversine distance to the nearest of seven Neolithic origin centres, migratory distance from Addis Ababa, a landlocked dummy, log land area, a centroid-based ruggedness proxy, mean pre-industrial temperature, and absolute latitude. Added sequentially, they leave the $\sigma_v^T \to \log$ pop growth coefficient remarkably stable.

Distance to Neolithic centres does itself distinguish pathways in a one-way ANOVA ($F=4.96$, $p=0.003$), but productive-months is the stronger Stage~1 predictor ($F=6.31$, $p<10^{-3}$); in a joint multinomial logit, productive-months retains independent significance ($p=0.001$ in the crop-dominant equation) after controlling for Neolithic distance. Interacting Sigl-Toohey VSSI with standardised log distance to Neolithic centres in the volcanic regression returns an interaction coefficient of $-3.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ with $p=0.57$: the pathway-heterogeneous volcanic response is not driven by countries' deep-historical exposure to agriculture.

\section{A calibrated quantitative model}
\label{app:calibrated-model}

To organise the reduced-form coefficients into a forward-simulation framework and to compute counterfactuals, we calibrate a parsimonious pathway-specific dynamic model. The model takes the empirical pathway-stratified coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:stage2} as structural inputs rather than estimating deep production-function parameters that the data do not identify; we accordingly call this a \emph{calibrated quantitative model} rather than a structural estimation.

For country $i$ in pathway $\tau$ and HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$, annualised log population growth follows
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln N_{it} = \alpha_\tau + \beta_\tau(t)\, \tilde d_{it} + \gamma_\tau\, \tilde T_{it} + \delta_\tau\, \tilde\sigma^T_{it} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:struct}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde d, \tilde T, \tilde\sigma^T$ are within-country deviations from the pre-industrial country mean and the Malthusian density coefficient evolves over time as $\beta_\tau(t) = \beta_{0,\tau} + \eta_\tau (t - 1421)/100$. The slope $\eta_\tau$ captures the rate at which the Malthusian feedback weakens with the secular accumulation of human capital, literacy, and storage, calibrated from how fast $\hat\beta_\tau$ moves toward zero across our three subperiods.

\midrule
Crop-dominant late      & $+0.031$ & $+0.0043$ & $-0.0029$ & $-0.0021$ & $-0.0057$ \\
Pastoral/mixed late     & $-0.028$ & $-0.0054$ & $+0.0016$ & $+0.0038$ & $+0.0037$ \\

\end{table}

\textbf{Validation.} The model captures within-country growth deviations modestly: pseudo-$R^2$ in one-step-ahead predictions ranges from $0.26$ (early extensifiers) to $-0.18$ (high-density intensive). The model does \emph{not} reproduce the cross-country long-shadow result quantitatively: simulating terminal cumulative growth and regressing on pre-industrial $\sigma_v^T$ returns a non-significant coefficient ($\hat\beta^{\text{sim}}=+0.18$, $p=0.56$). We read this failure as informative rather than fatal: the cross-country long shadow operates primarily through institutional and technological mediators that the within-country Malthusian dynamics do not capture, consistent with the institutional-channel interpretation we already gave in Section~\ref{sec:shadow}.

\textbf{Counterfactual experiments.} The model's value is for counterfactual computations the empirical regressions cannot perform directly. CF1: re-simulate each pastoral/mixed-late country under the calibrated parameters of the high-density-intensive pathway, keeping its actual climate trajectory. Median cumulative pre-industrial growth lift: $+0.197$ log-units, equivalent to a $+21.8\%$ population gain ($N=65$ countries; IQR $+7.1\%$ to $+33.0\%$). CF2: set within-interval $\sigma^T$ to zero in every HYDE interval that contains a $\geq 5$~Tg Sigl-Toohey eruption 1500--1900. Cumulative population effect: $+23.2\%$ crop-dominant, $-15.1\%$ pastoral/mixed (positive $\delta_T$: their mobility hedge benefits from volatility), $+29.1\%$ high-density intensive, $+22.7\%$ early extensifiers. The sign asymmetry across pathways is what the model exists to quantify; it would not have been recoverable from the reduced-form regressions alone. CF3: shrink within-interval $\sigma^T$ by a fixed weight $w \in \{1.0, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.0\}$ for all countries and recompute cumulative pre-industrial growth.

Crop-dominant, high-density intensive, and early-extensifiers gain (negative $\delta_T$); pastoral loses (positive $\delta_T$). (b) Mean cumulative growth deviation as the volatility weight $w$ shrinks from 1 to 0.}

\textbf{Welfare counterfactuals via Allen wages.} To translate population into welfare, we use the Allen real-wage Malthus regression coefficients from Section~\ref{sec:allen}: $\hat\beta_{\ln \text{pop}} = -0.285$ on lagged log population. Per-capita welfare and total welfare in each counterfactual are
$\Delta \ln w_{\tau}^{\text{per cap}} = \hat\beta_{\ln \text{pop}} \cdot \Delta \ln N_\tau$ and $\Delta W_{\tau}^{\text{total}} = \Delta \ln N_\tau + \Delta \ln w_\tau^{\text{per cap}}$.

The canonical Malthusian welfare paradox emerges: removing volcanic forcing increases the headcount but reduces per-capita real wages. For high-density intensive systems, removing 1500--1900 volcanism produces $+29\%$ more people each $-6.9\%$ poorer on average---a net utilitarian gain of $+0.18$ log-welfare but a clearly worse outcome for the representative individual in the affected centuries. The pastoral exception is informative: removing volcanism reduces population in pastoral systems (positive $\delta_T$), so volatility helps their growth, and per-capita wages rise, but total welfare falls because there are fewer people. The exercise is not a deep structural estimation. Two pieces of information come out cleanly: (i) the order-of-magnitude demographic effect of agricultural-pathway choice (about $\pm 20$--$30\%$ population over the pre-industrial period for plausibly-comparable countries), and (ii) the order of magnitude of cumulative volcanic forcing across 400 years ($\pm 15$--$29\%$ population by pathway). The model treats population as the single endogenous outcome and is silent on the joint cropland-and-urbanization dynamics that Section~\ref{sec:joint-var} surfaces; an extension to a three-equation forward simulation is a natural next step.
