\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Using a new monthly paleo-economic panel of 196 countries and 3{,}187 sub-national units from 1421~CE, we identify the pre-industrial Malthusian demographic response to volcanic forcing and place it alongside two further findings. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late countries lose population at $-9.7$ and $-5.5\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg of stratospheric sulfur injection respectively, Bonferroni-significant and robust across every stress test we run; mortality data 1751--1900 trace the channel to the positive check on infant and child mortality, with the response collapsing to zero by 1950 as sanitation develops. Pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 is the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth ($R^2=0.45$); a growing-season decomposition of the channel shows the predictive content is concentrated in non-growing-season variance ($|\hat\beta_{\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}|$ exceeds $|\hat\beta_{\mathrm{GS}}|$ by 2$\times$ to 17$\times$ across nine specification cells), so the long shadow is climate-deep rather than agronomically specific. On HYDE the Boserupian cropland-margin test fails on two distinct measurement axes; on the population-independent KK10 reconstruction \citep{kaplan2011} a small positive Boserupian signal emerges that survives the demographic-margin control, two orders below what the HYDE specification had implied.

\section{Introduction}

In April 1815, Mount Tambora ejected roughly 60 teragrams of sulfur into the stratosphere---the largest historically documented eruption---and triggered the ``year without a summer'' of 1816 across the Northern Hemisphere. Crops failed across continental Europe and New England; wheat prices in Paris rose nearly threefold; what contemporaries called ``famine fever'' (typhus and typhoid) killed an estimated 200{,}000 people across Ireland, Switzerland, and Italy alone \citep{wood2014year}. Tambora is not an outlier in the deeper historical record. Samalas 1257 (eVolv2k VSSI $\approx 59$~Tg) preceded the Great Famine of 1315--1317; the 1452--1453 Kuwae eruption coincided with sustained European grain-price elevation; the 540~CE LALIA doublet sits at the onset of the late-antique European population contraction \citep{buntgen2016cooling}. Volcanic eruptions are the cleanest exogenous climate shock the historical record contains: tectonic forcing that injects stratospheric sulfate, cools the surface for one to three years, and reduces agricultural yields in a way that is uncorrelated with prior economic conditions. The question this paper asks is how pre-industrial economies absorbed such shocks, and which economies absorbed them how.

The theoretical answers are two centuries old and pull in opposite directions. \citet{malthus1798} described a demographic adjustment: when climate stress reduces food production per capita, population contracts through the positive check (mortality) and, more slowly, through the preventive check (fertility postponement). \citet{boserup1965}, by contrast, argued that the demographic adjustment was at best a partial response. Faced with land-pressure shocks, cultivators expanded the cultivated area, shortened fallow rotations, and adopted more labour-intensive techniques: an agricultural-margin adjustment that absorbed the shock without contracting population. The two responses are not mutually exclusive in principle---an economy can lose people and intensify cultivation simultaneously---but they make different predictions about which margin a country relies on, and Boserup explicitly objected to the Malthusian framing's silence on the agricultural-margin alternative.

Empirically, the field has had to choose its battles carefully because the data on pre-industrial climate, population, and land use have not aligned cleanly. The macroeconomics-of-climate literature \citep{dell2012temperature, burke2015global, hsiang2017global} has identified large first-order temperature effects on output and population on the modern instrumental record (1950--), but the modern record cannot speak to the pre-industrial Malthusian regime that ended decades earlier and that Galor's unified growth theory \citep{galor2011unified, galor2000population} has placed at the centre of long-run growth. The pre-industrial Malthusian econometrics tradition \citep{ashraf2011dynamics, voigtlander2013how, clark2007farewell} has estimated the strength of the Malthusian feedback from historical population panels, but has had to treat climate forcing as a residual inside country fixed effects because annual country-level climate has not been measured before the 20th century. The climate-historical literature \citep{buntgen2016cooling, weiss2008climate, pages2k2019} has identified famine events around large eruptions but has not been able to support large-$N$ panel inference. The Neolithic-foundations literature \citep{matranga2024, olsson2005, diamond1997guns} has established that pre-industrial climate endowments shaped which agricultural systems societies adopted, but has stopped short of asking whether the same endowments shape climate-shock responses. The Boserupian intensification claim, perhaps the most consequential alternative to the Malthusian baseline, has remained largely untested at the cross-country pre-industrial scale because no panel has resolved cropland-and-population-and-climate jointly at the resolution the test requires.

This paper assembles such a panel and runs the test. We aggregate the ModE-RA paleo-reanalysis \citep{franke2023modera}---a monthly global assimilation of documentary records, tree rings, ice cores, and historical instruments---onto HYDE 3.5 land-use cells \citep{goldewijk2017}, calibrate against the modern ERA5 instrumental record \citep{hersbach2020era5}, and link the result to the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k ice-core record of stratospheric sulfur injection \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017}, the Brecke conflict catalogue \citep{brecke1999}, the Allen-Nuffield wage-and-price archive \citep{allen2001divergence, malanima2002}, and the Human Mortality / Human Fertility Databases. The resulting panel covers 196 countries and 3{,}187 sub-national units back to 1421~CE at country-monthly resolution---a measurement unit that, to our knowledge, has not previously been available for econometric inference. Volcanic eruptions provide the identifying variation: 1500--1900 contains roughly sixty distinct eruption years with stratospheric sulfur injection above the noise floor of the eVolv2k record, and the decadal HYDE temporal grid is fine enough to resolve their demographic and land-use consequences.

The empirical work yields three findings.

\emph{First, the Malthusian demographic margin is sharply pathway-heterogeneous and identifiable.} Estimating a joint three-equation panel VAR of population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth on lagged levels, contemporaneous interval climate, and decade-summed VSSI exposure, crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late countries show population-growth coefficients of $-9.7$ and $-5.5\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg respectively, both Bonferroni-significant across twelve pathway-by-equation tests, both robust to a Webb wild-cluster bootstrap, both robust to re-clustering pathways on climate primitives orthogonal to HYDE outcomes, and both surviving restriction to the post-1700 decadal portion of HYDE. A 30-Tg Tambora-class decade translates to roughly $3\%$ cumulative population loss in crop-dominant late countries; a 10-Tg ``ordinary'' eruption decade implies $\sim 1\%$. The annual European Human Mortality / Human Fertility Database panel covering ten countries 1751--1900 traces the channel to the positive check on infant and child mortality, with the response collapsing to zero by 1950--2022 as sanitation develops---the sanitation-mediated reversal that the historical demography literature \citep{wrigley1981} predicts but that has not previously been quantified with paleo-reanalysis climate forcing. The Allen real-wage panel for six European countries 1421--1850 returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution---the welfare-side echo of the same channel, with the implied real-wage rise after a Tambora-class population contraction $\sim +0.95\%$ for the survivors.

\emph{Second, the pre-industrial climate regime that selected agricultural pathways leaves a long shadow on modern demographic outcomes.} Pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 is the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth at $R^2 = 0.45$, surviving both latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery (Neolithic distance, migratory distance from Addis Ababa, ruggedness, log land area, mean pre-industrial temperature) and failing a placebo when $\sigma_v^T$ is computed over the modern 1950--2008 reanalysis window ($p = 0.16$). The result is specific to demographic outcomes---modern $\Delta$ urban share and modern log GDP per capita are absorbed by latitude---and aligns with the institutional-development literature \citep{ashraf2013out, putterman2008, andersen2017climate}: the climate dimension that selected which adjustment margin a society relied on before 1750 leaves traces in modern demographic trajectories that are not contained in either modern climate or geographic deep determinants.

\emph{Third, the Boserupian intensification hypothesis identifies only on a population-independent land-use reconstruction.} The natural HYDE test---cropland share as the outcome---returns a nominally positive coefficient ($+7.8\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ per Tg, $p=0.003$ in the 1800--1900 sub-sample) but fails on two distinct measurement axes. The cropland-share specification is artefactually positive: the coefficient is driven by HYDE colonial-transition encoding in 24 small-island and tropical-Africa countries; it vanishes when we restrict to the substantively-named 21-country Mediterranean / Eurasian-steppe / SE-Asia core, attenuates tenfold under population weighting, and decomposes into a cropland-area contraction (the opposite sign of intensification) plus a faster grazing-area contraction. The fallback cropland-area specification is statistically robust on the substantive sample but is fully mediated by the demographic margin, because HYDE's pre-1850 reconstruction takes country-level population as an input \citep{goldewijk2017, koch2019}: adding $\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P$ as a control collapses the area coefficient to zero in every specification we run. Cross-validating against the independent KK10 reconstruction of \citet{kaplan2011}---methodologically population-independent---surfaces a small positive Boserupian signal that does survive the demographic-margin control: $+1.07\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg on the 21-country core ($p=0.005$), $+4.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ on the high-density intensive pathway ($p=0.047$). The Boserupian channel exists at pre-industrial cross-country scale, but at a magnitude two orders below what the HYDE specification had originally implied---accounting for at most $\sim 1.3\%$ cumulative anthropogenic-land expansion over a Tambora-class decade.

\emph{Contributions.} Two methodological contributions accompany the substantive findings: the monthly country-resolution paleo-economic panel itself (a measurement unit not previously available to econometric inference on the pre-industrial period), and the documentation of HYDE's two-axis identification problem along with the KK10 cross-reconstruction strategy as the resolution. The paper proceeds as follows: Section~\ref{sec:data} introduces the panel and the agricultural-pathway typology; Section~\ref{sec:identification} works out the within-country identification strategy and the Malthusian baseline; Section~\ref{sec:headline} delivers the three main empirical exercises; Section~\ref{sec:shadow} runs the long-shadow regression; Section~\ref{sec:robustness} consolidates robustness; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{A new monthly paleo-economic panel}
\label{sec:data}

The paper rests on a paleo-economic dataset assembled for the first time. We describe its construction, validate its alignment with the modern instrumental record where they overlap, and use it to characterise the climate-pathway typology that organises the rest of the paper.

\subsection{Construction}

The dataset has three substantive layers and three event layers merged onto a common country-year (or city-year, or sub-unit-year) grid. HYDE~3.5 \citep{goldewijk2017} provides gridded population, cropland, grazing, irrigated, rice, and urban-population estimates at 5-arcminute resolution; we aggregate to 196 country totals and 3{,}187 sub-national units. The HYDE temporal grid is irregular (100-year intervals before 1700, decadal 1700--1950, annual thereafter); the decadal sample powers most regressions. ModE-RA \citep{franke2023modera} is a monthly global paleo-reanalysis covering 1421--2008~CE that assimilates documentary records, tree rings, ice cores, corals, and historical instruments into a coupled numerical-model background; we aggregate it onto HYDE country and sub-national units via cosine-latitude-weighted area sums and add back the CRU~TS~4.09 \citep{harris2020cruts} 1901--1950 country-month climatology to obtain absolute temperatures and precipitation throughout 1421--2008. We rebuild ERA5 \citep{hersbach2020era5} at the country level analogously, with per-country mean bias removed by a constant offset estimated in the 1950--2008 overlap with ModE-RA.

Three derived climate measures play headline roles: intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T(i,t) = \max_m T_{i,t,m} - \min_m T_{i,t,m}$; the productive-months index $\mathrm{Prod}_{i,t}$ counting calendar months simultaneously $5^\circ\mathrm{C} \le T \le 30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $P \ge 30~\mathrm{mm}$ (warm and wet enough for rain-fed cropping), whose complement captures storage demand; and inter-annual temperature volatility $\sigma_v^T(i,t)$ as the country-specific standard deviation of annual mean temperature in a 30-year centred window. The productive-month criterion also defines a fixed \emph{growing-season mask} $\mathrm{GS}_i \equiv \{m : 5{\le} \bar T^{\mathrm{clim}}_{i,m} {\le} 30 \text{ and } \bar P^{\mathrm{clim}}_{i,m} {\ge} 30~\mathrm{mm}\}$, with monthly climatology computed once over the 1421--1750 cropland-weighted ModE-RA panel. The mask supports the GS-vs-non-GS decomposition of the long-shadow channel in Section~\ref{sec:shadow}, where we test whether the predictive power of $\sigma_v^T$ is agronomically specific to the growing season.

Three event layers attach to specific (country, year) or (city, year) cells. The Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k v4 ice-core record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017} provides annual stratospheric sulfur injection from 500~BCE to 1900~CE with eruption latitudes. The Brecke Conflict Catalogue \citep{brecke1999} adds 3{,}708 violent conflicts 1400--1999 plus 1{,}147 European conflicts 900--1402; we string-match actor descriptions to medieval regions and hand-code 30 sieges and 49 plague episodes. The Allen-Nuffield archive \citep{allen2001divergence} contributes silver-content-normalised wheat prices for 17 European cities 1259--1914 and real wages for 8 cities 1264--1850, augmented with Tuscan prices from \citet{malanima2002} and the Roman-Egypt wheat-price series of \citet{harper2016people}.

\subsection{Validation}

The country-level monthly climate panel matches the modern instrumental record in the 1950--2008 ModE-RA/ERA5 overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}): median country-month mean bias $0.61^\circ$C, median country-monthly $T$ correlation $0.971$ (77.6\% of countries exceed $r=0.90$), median country-annual anomaly correlation $0.938$ after detrending. ModE-RA's ensemble $1\sigma$ uncertainty in country-month temperature falls from $0.9^\circ$C in 1421--1500 to $0.37^\circ$C by 1850--1949; precipitation uncertainty falls from $31.6$ to $15.4$~mm/month. Past 1950 we splice in the country-level ERA5 rebuild via the bias-correction step described above. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble} (Appendix~\ref{app:ensemble-uncertainty}) reports the merged-panel uncertainty across 1421--2025.

The panel also captures documented climate-driven famines: negative temperature anomalies during the Great Frost of 1709 and the Tambora year-without-a-summer 1816, the cold-and-wet anomaly during the Irish potato famine 1845--49, and the precipitation deficits during the North China famine 1876--79 and the Bengal famine 1943 all show up in the panel at the relevant country and decade.

\subsection{Five agricultural pathways}
\label{sec:pathways}

The new panel discriminates a typology of agricultural development that the existing literature has discussed informally but not measured systematically. Applying $K$-means clustering to standardised HYDE trajectory features---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, 1000~CE, and 0~CE, crop share at 1750, rice share at 1750, irrigation share at 1750, urban share at 1750, two interval population-growth rates, and an agricultural-intensity change index---returns a five-cluster partition. The choice of $K=5$ is the silhouette local maximum on this feature set (0.40 at $K=5$, with a sharp drop to 0.23 at $K=6$); bootstrap stability of the partition is 0.84 in median country-pair co-clustering probability across 200 resamples. The five pathways are high-density intensive (Japan, India, France, Germany, Netherlands, 16 countries), early extensifiers (Britain, Switzerland, Austria, Denmark, Israel, 29 countries), crop-dominant late (Spain, Poland, Portugal, Thailand, 46 countries), pastoral/mixed late (China, Nigeria, Brazil, Iran, Mexico, 65 countries), and the irrigation pioneer Egypt as a singleton (Table~\ref{tab:pathways}). The pathways differ along all five clustering dimensions---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, crop share, irrigation share, and rate of demographic expansion---in ways that match the historical literature's informal taxonomy.\footnote{One robustness concern bears explicit disclosure. HYDE~3.5's pre-industrial Americas cropland and population fields are partially mechanical back-projections from the \citet{koch2019} colonial-era population estimates; independent reconstructions such as KK10 \citep{kaplan2011} differ from HYDE on the magnitude of post-1492 Americas land-use change. The Americas countries in our panel (Brazil, Mexico, and other members of the pastoral/mixed late cluster) inherit any HYDE-mechanical-encoding bias in their clustering-feature values. We address the concern through the climate-only re-clustering exercise of Appendix~\ref{app:joint-var-climate-clusters}, which assigns countries to clusters using exclusively pre-industrial climate primitives (productive months, intra-annual $T$ and $P$ range, mean climate, inter-annual $T$ volatility) that are orthogonal to HYDE outcomes; the demographic-margin VSSI coefficient survives the re-clustering, the off-margin coefficients do not. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reads the implications, and Section~\ref{sec:boserup} reports a fifth, non-nested Boserup sub-sample that drops crop-dominant late countries with significant exposure to the post-1492 Americas back-projection.}

\subsection{Climate signatures of the pathways}
\label{sec:stage1}

The natural Matranga measure---raw intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T$ averaged over 1421--1750---does not significantly distinguish pathways at the country level (ANOVA $F=0.50$, $p=0.68$). What does distinguish pathways is the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that determines storage demand. The productive-months index differs sharply across pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:storage}): crop-dominant late and high-density intensive have 9.5 and 9.1 productive months on average; pastoral/mixed late has 7.0; early extensifiers 7.7; the singleton irrigation pioneer (Egypt) has zero rain-fed productive months. A one-way ANOVA on productive months returns $F=6.31$, $p<0.001$, and a multinomial logit with the productive-months index and the storage index $(12-\mathrm{Prod})\cdot \sigma_s^T/12$ attains pseudo-$R^2=0.065$ and LLR $p=0.0003$. The Matranga storage-demand story survives, but it operates through the joint structure of temperature and precipitation rather than through temperature variability alone.

\section{From storage demand to the Malthusian regression}
\label{sec:identification}

The empirical work that follows is organised around the within-country identification strategy that the appendix model motivates. Appendix~\ref{app:theory} sketches a two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics} that delivers two empirical predictions. First, in a society's far past, the share of climatically productive months $\Pi$ jointly with inter-annual climate volatility $\sigma_v$ selects which agricultural system $\tau \in \{$intensive crop, extensive pastoral, irrigation-based$\}$ the society adopts; the model produces a productive-months threshold $\Pi^*(\sigma_v)$ above which intensive crop dominates pastoral (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}). Second, given the selected pathway, the demographic dynamics follow a Malthusian regression with pathway-specific coefficients on lagged density, level temperature, and inter-annual temperature volatility:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \ln P_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta(\tau^\ast)\, d_{i,t-1} \;+\; \gamma(\tau^\ast)\, T_{it} \;+\; \delta(\tau^\ast)\, \sigma_v^T(T_{it}) \;+\; \eta(\tau^\ast)\, h_{it} \;+\; \varepsilon_{it}.
  \label{eq:malthus-main}
\end{equation}
This is the regression we run. The Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted stronger in pastoral than in intensive systems---pastoral systems sit closer to a tight ceiling because they cannot store, intensive systems carry capital that smooths the feedback---and the volatility coefficient $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted to be larger in magnitude for pastoral systems, whose thinner safety nets do not buffer year-to-year shocks. The level-temperature coefficient $\gamma(\tau^\ast)$ admits both signs depending on whether the baseline climate is at the warm or cool end of the productive range; we treat its empirical sign as informative, not predicted. The human-capital coefficient $\eta(\tau^\ast)$ is the rate at which the Malthusian feedback weakens with secular accumulation of literacy, urbanisation, and capital, and is the channel through which the demographic transition appears in our sub-period regressions.

We take the appendix model as motivational, not literal. The full calibrated quantitative exercise (Appendix~\ref{app:calibrated-model}) takes the model's pathway-specific parameters from the reduced-form coefficients estimated here and uses the calibrated system for counterfactual simulations rather than for inference. Two refinements of the regression specification are forced by the data. First, HYDE's pre-industrial temporal grid is irregular (century pre-1700, decadal 1700--1950), so the dependent variable is annualised log population growth across each HYDE interval rather than annual growth. Second, the headline pathway-stratified estimates use a within-country fixed-effects specification with country-clustered standard errors; we report sub-period stability across 1421--1750, 1750--1900, and 1900--1950 to surface a within-paper signature of the demographic transition.

\subsection{Sub-national identification of the storage-demand channel}
\label{sec:subnational}

Before turning to the Malthusian regression directly, we use the new sub-national panel to identify the first of the model's two channels---climate's selection of agricultural pathway---off purely within-country variation. The country-level Stage 1 regressions in Section~\ref{sec:pathways} cannot rule out unobserved country-level confounders (legal institutions, historical conquest, distance to coast) that could drive both pathway and climate measures spuriously. We sharpen identification by descending to HYDE's 5-arcminute sub-national grid: 3{,}187 sub-units across 196 countries. We aggregate ModE-RA monthly fields onto each sub-unit by the same area-weighting procedure used for countries, compute the pre-industrial productive-months index per sub-unit, and run within-country regressions of HYDE agricultural outcomes on the productive-months index with country fixed effects.

The country fixed effect absorbs every country-level confounder. The remaining variation is purely intra-national. To the extent that the storage-demand mechanism operates through climate \emph{within} a country, this specification identifies it cleanly. Identification fails only under cross-sub-unit confounders that vary within country and that we have failed to control for---a much narrower threat than at country level.

Table~\ref{tab:subnational} reports the within-country regressions of crop share and log population density on productive months at three HYDE timesteps. At 1750, sub-units with one more productive month per year are 6.7~pp higher in crop share ($p=0.019$) and 0.66 log-units higher in log population density ($p<10^{-3}$). The within-country point estimates are 3.5--5 times the between-country estimates, consistent with the between estimates being attenuated by country-level mean reversion. The within pattern is stable through 1850 and 1950.

\subsection{Malthusian dynamics with real annual climate, 1421--1950}
\label{sec:stage2}

Pre-industrial Malthusian regressions in the existing literature \citep{ashraf2011dynamics} have been run without explicit climate controls because suitable climate forcing has not been available at country-year resolution back to the pre-industrial period. The ModE-RA panel makes that possible. For each (country $i$, HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$) in the 1421--1950 sample, we compute the interval-mean temperature anomaly $\bar T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ and precipitation anomaly $\bar P_{i,[t,t+1)}$ relative to the country mean, the within-interval $T$ standard deviation $\sigma^T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ as a measure of inter-annual volatility, and the analogous precipitation volatility. The estimating equation, the empirical analogue of (\ref{eq:malthus-main}), is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,[t,t+1)} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta\, \ln d_{it} \;+\; \gamma_T \bar T \;+\; \gamma_P \bar P \;+\; \delta_T \sigma^T \;+\; \delta_P \sigma^P \;+\; \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:malthus2}
\end{equation}
estimated by within-country fixed effects with country-clustered standard errors.

\subsubsection{Pathway-stratified estimates, 1421--1750}

\section{Volcanic forcing and the Malthusian channel}
\label{sec:headline}

Volcanic eruptions provide climate forcing that is exogenous to economic activity by construction: tectonic forcing injects stratospheric sulfate, the sulfate cools the surface in spatially heterogeneous patterns over the subsequent one-to-three years, and the cooling reduces agricultural yields in a way that is uncorrelated with prior economic conditions. The Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k v4 ice-core record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017} catalogues every major eruption from 500~BCE to 1900~CE with its stratospheric sulfur injection (VSSI, in Tg); summed at the decadal interval of the HYDE panel, $V_{it}$ delivers the exogenous identifying variation we need.

This section identifies the pathway-heterogeneous demographic response to volcanic forcing as the lead empirical result (\S\ref{sec:joint-var}), reproduces it on single-equation pathway-stratified regressions and extends the price-side margin to the 1257 Samalas eruption (\S\ref{sec:volcanic}), corroborates the demographic side with welfare-margin evidence from the Allen real-wage archive (\S\ref{sec:allen}) and a positive-vs-preventive mechanism decomposition from European vital statistics (\S\ref{sec:prevpos}), and reports an honest null on the cropland-side Boserupian intensification margin (\S\ref{sec:boserup}). The headline finding is a Bonferroni-significant, multiply-robust, pathway-heterogeneous demographic response; the supporting evidence is the welfare and mortality decomposition; the cropland exercise reports a measurement-driven artifact that does not survive the substantive country-sample restriction.

\subsection{Pathway-heterogeneous demographic margin: a joint three-equation panel VAR}
\label{sec:joint-var}

We estimate a three-equation panel VAR of population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth on lagged levels, contemporaneous interval-mean climate, within-interval temperature volatility, and decade-summed Sigl-Toohey volcanic stratospheric sulfur injection (VSSI) exposure $V_{it}$. The cropland-share and urban-share equations are placed alongside the demographic equation primarily as identification companions---to surface the simultaneity between the demographic margin and the other margins of pre-industrial adjustment---rather than as substantive co-equal channels. For each country $i$ and HYDE decadal interval $t$ in 1500--1900:
\begin{align}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,t}      &= \alpha^P_i + \phi^P_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^P_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^P_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^P_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^P V_{it} + \varepsilon^P_{it}, \label{eq:joint-pop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln s_{i,t}      &= \alpha^C_i + \phi^C_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^C_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^C_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^C_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^C V_{it} + \varepsilon^C_{it}, \label{eq:joint-crop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln u_{i,t}      &= \alpha^U_i + \phi^U_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^U_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^U_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^U_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^U V_{it} + \varepsilon^U_{it}, \label{eq:joint-urb}
\end{align}
where $s_{i,t}$ is the cropland share of agricultural land (cropland / (cropland + grazing)) and $u_{i,t}$ is the urban share of total population. Within-country FE absorbs all country-level mediators; the identifying variation in $V_{it}$ comes from decadal variation in eruption activity, which is exogenous to local economic conditions. SEs are clustered by country.

The pathway-stratified version (Figure~\ref{fig:joint-var}) shows the demographic margin operating differentially across pathways. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late pathways show large negative demographic coefficients ($-9.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p<10^{-6}$ and $-5.5\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p<10^{-5}$ respectively on $\Delta \log P$), both Bonferroni-significant across the twelve pathway-by-equation tests, both robust to re-clustering on climate primitives orthogonal to HYDE, and both surviving restriction to the post-1700 decadal portion of HYDE where the demographic back-projection is less dependent on land-use imputation (Appendix~\ref{app:joint-var-robustness}). \subsection{Single-equation cross-checks and pre-XV-century extension}
\label{sec:volcanic}

The single-equation pathway-stratified version of the joint specification---$\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P$ on country FE and VSSI alone---reproduces the demographic-margin coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:joint-var}: crop-dominant systems $-9.7\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg ($p<10^{-9}$), pastoral $-7.2\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p<10^{-11}$), early extensifiers $-4.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p<10^{-6}$), high-density intensive a near-zero $-1.2\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p=0.55$). A dynamic IRF at decade leads and lags passes parallel trends ($F=0.33$ on the two leads, $p=0.72$) and peaks at $h=+2$ decades ($\hat\beta = -36\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p<10^{-4}$). A pre-XV-century extension via the Allen-Studer European wheat-price panel (1259--1500, nine cities) and the Harper (2016) Roman-Egypt wheat-price series (45--650~CE) recovers a supply-side spike peaking two years post-eruption ($+0.0089$ per Tg, $p=0.10$); applied to the 59~Tg Samalas eruption of 1257, this implies a $\approx 52\%$ price spike by 1259, in the right neighbourhood of the Great Famine of 1315--1317. Twenty-five-year-bin event studies around Samalas and around the 540~CE LALIA doublet recover the canonical post-famine demand-side aftermath; the pathway-heterogeneous demographic test does not survive translation to century-binned HYDE intervals pre-1500. Details in Appendix~\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500}.

\subsection{Welfare evidence: real wages and the city-buffer climate}
\label{sec:allen}

Population is a smoothed proxy for welfare. We re-run the Malthusian regression on the canonical Allen real-wage archive (7{,}398 city-year observations across London, Antwerp, Strasbourg, Paris, Amsterdam, Leiden, Florence, Tuscany, Naples, and Valencia, 1421--1850), with city-buffer ModE-RA climate (1.5$^\circ$ box around each city centroid) and city fixed effects. The lagged-population coefficient is $-0.325$ ($p=0.058$), in line with the Malthusian prediction; warmer years raise real wages ($\hat\gamma_T = +0.025$, $p<10^{-3}$), the welfare-side echo of the demographic response in the headline regression (Table~\ref{tab:allen}, Spec~4). The Allen Malthus coefficient $\hat\beta_{\ln P} = -0.325$ translates the demographic-margin volcanic response into a per-capita welfare statement. A 30~Tg Tambora-class decade implies a $\sim 3\%$ cumulative population decline in crop-dominant late countries, which through the Allen lagged-population coefficient maps to a $\sim +0.95\%$ real-wage rise for the survivors---the canonical Malthusian welfare paradox at a magnitude small relative to the documented post-Black-Death real-wage rise of $+28$ to $+44\%$ \citep{wrigley1981, clark2007farewell} but structurally identical: an exogenous mortality shock thins the population, the land-to-labour ratio rises, and per-capita output rises with it. The welfare cost of pre-industrial volcanic forcing is not the dead but the unborn: the dead leave wages higher for the survivors; volcanic eruptions cost cumulative welfare-per-capita through the missing population that would have been born in the eruption's absence. Appendix~\ref{app:calibrated-model}'s no-volcanism counterfactual quantifies this at $-0.32$ log-units of cumulative per-capita welfare loss for the median crop-dominant late country 1500--1890.

\subsection{Preventive and positive Malthusian checks}
\label{sec:prevpos}

The decadal HYDE panel cannot decompose the climate-Malthus response into the preventive check (fertility postponement) and the positive check (mortality), because the difference between births and deaths is not observed at country-decade resolution. We answer the decomposition on a 547-observation Human Mortality / Human Fertility Database annual panel covering ten European countries 1751--1900, with country FE, country-linear trends, and country-clustered SEs. Annual warm-year shocks operate primarily through the positive check: $+1\,^\circ$C raises $\log m_0$ by $+2.35\%$ ($p=0.009$), $\log m_5$ by $+2.58\%$ ($p=0.003$), and $\log m_{10}$ by $+2.56\%$ ($p=0.002$), while the preventive check on log fertility is small and insignificant ($+0.75\%$, $p=0.14$). Precipitation is the mirror image: drier years carry higher mortality. This is the historically well-documented ``summer mortality'' pattern of pre-modern Europe \citep{wrigley1981}: in the absence of refrigeration and sanitation, warm summers triggered dysentery and gastroenteritis outbreaks that disproportionately killed infants. The channel is concentrated in the high-density intensive pathway ($+5.3\%$ per $^\circ$C on $\log m_0$, $p<10^{-6}$) and weakens monotonically through extensifiers and pastoral systems, scaling with population density as the infectious-disease mechanism predicts. The $m_0$ coefficient falls from $+0.0235$ per $^\circ$C in 1751--1900 to $+0.0003$ in 1950--2022 (a 75-fold reduction, $p=0.84$)---the sanitation-mediated reversal the historical literature documents. Pathway-stratified, modern-era, and English-parish-aggregate extensions are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:prevpos-extensions}.

\subsection{Cropland response to volcanic forcing: an honest null}
\label{sec:boserup}

\citet{boserup1965} hypothesised that pre-industrial populations facing climate-induced yield shocks would respond on the agricultural extensive margin (more land under cultivation, less fallow rotation) rather than only contracting demographically. The hypothesis is the structural opposite of the Malthusian one on the cropland axis. We test it with the cropland-share specification
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln s_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \phi^s_{\tau(i)}\, V_{it} + \mathbf{x}_{it}' \boldsymbol{\theta} + \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:boserup}
\end{equation}
where $s_{i,t}$ is the cropland share of agricultural land (cropland / (cropland + grazing)) and the joint-VAR control vector $\mathbf{x}_{it}$ is the same as in Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-crop}. The headline finding is a measurement-driven artifact, and we report it as such here---the disclosure is itself useful, since HYDE-based cropland-share regressions appear elsewhere in the literature without this diagnostic.

The 45-country crop-dominant late panel returns a nominally positive coefficient that strengthens monotonically as the sample contracts toward the modern instrumental era. In the full 1500--1900 panel the coefficient is small and insignificant ($+3.0\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg, $p=0.258$); restricting to year~$\ge 1700$ it jumps to $+3.6\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ ($p=0.031$); 1750--1900 strengthens to $+3.9\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ ($p=0.034$); 1800--1900 tightens to $+7.8\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ ($p=0.003$) on 45 countries and 450 country-decades (Table~\ref{tab:boserup-subsamples}). The other three pathways show no comparable positive response in any post-1700 window. A Cochran $Q$ test on a non-overlapping split (1700--1750, 1750--1800, 1800--1900) rejects coefficient homogeneity across the three windows ($Q=10.83$ on 2 d.f., $p=0.004$; $I^2 = 82\%$): the apparent monotonic tightening is not a stable post-1700 phenomenon but is concentrated entirely in 1800--1900 (the two earlier non-overlapping windows return $+3.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ at $p=0.15$ and a precisely-estimated zero respectively).

The cropland-share estimate fails three measurement diagnostics, reported in detail in Appendix~\ref{app:cropland-diagnostics}. First, decomposing the cropland share into its numerator and denominator surfaces an inversion: in the 1800--1900 sample log cropland \emph{area} contracts at $-9.6\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg ($p=0.08$) and log grazing area contracts $70\times$ faster ($-7.0\!\cdot\!10^{-3}$ per Tg, $p=0.002$); cropland share rises only because the denominator falls faster than the numerator. Second, restricting the sample to the substantively-named 21-country Mediterranean / Eurasian-steppe / SE-Asia core---and dropping 24 small-island and tropical-Africa countries whose HYDE cropland fields are partly mechanical back-projections from colonial-era population estimates---collapses every cropland-margin coefficient to zero. Third, population-weighted estimation shrinks the headline 1800--1900 coefficient tenfold ($+7.8\!\cdot\!10^{-4}\to+6.1\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p=0.10$).

A natural fallback specification on the absolute cropland-area outcome ($\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln(\text{cropland km}^2)$) is statistically robust on the substantive sample but is fully mediated by the demographic margin: adding $\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,t}$ as a control collapses the area coefficient to a near-zero, statistically insignificant value across every pathway and window. In the 21-country crop-dominant late core post-1700 the baseline $-6.1\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p=0.001$) becomes $+8\!\cdot\!10^{-7}$ ($p=0.96$). The mechanical explanation is that HYDE's pre-1850 reconstruction uses country-level population as an input variable \citep{goldewijk2017}, so the area outcome is mechanically a function of the population covariate.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{KK10 cross-validation surfaces a small Boserupian signal.} We cross-validate against the independent KK10 reconstruction of \citet{kaplan2011}, whose anthropogenic-fraction series is methodologically population-independent: KK10 assigns land use as a function of climate-suitable agricultural area and density-varying per-capita scaling rather than as a function of national-level population. On the 21-country crop-dominant late core post-1700, the KK10 anthropogenic-area response to VSSI is $+1.07\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg ($p=0.005$) and survives the $\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P$ control essentially unchanged ($+1.08\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p=0.005$)---identifying the agricultural extensive margin in the sense the Boserupian hypothesis specifies. The HYDE-vs-KK10 disagreement on sign and orthogonality is itself diagnostic: HYDE algorithmically draws land and population together; KK10 does not. The Boserupian channel is real but small. A 30~Tg Tambora-class decade implies $\sim 0.32\%$ cumulative anthropogenic-land expansion in crop-dominant late countries---roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than the HYDE cropland-share specification artefactually suggested.

The pathway pattern is theoretically interesting. The high-density intensive pathway has the weakest demographic response to volcanic forcing (\S\ref{sec:joint-var}: $\hat\phi^P=-2.0\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $p=0.21$); KK10 now shows that intensive crop systems instead absorb shocks through anthropogenic-land expansion at $\hat\phi^L=+4.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$, $4\times$ larger than the crop-dominant late core. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late, where the demographic margin is strongest, show small or absent Boserupian responses; early extensifiers, which had already reached their agricultural frontier in the deep past, show neither channel. The pattern is qualitatively consistent with channel substitution---pathways trade off between margins---but a country-level errors-in-variables regression of the per-country Boserupian coefficient on the per-country demographic coefficient does not identify a substitution slope (Appendix~\ref{app:cropland-diagnostics}). We accordingly report the pattern as descriptive of the pathway-level magnitude ranking rather than as identification of a structural substitution elasticity.

The headline demographic finding does not depend on which land-use reconstruction we use for the lagged covariate. Swapping HYDE's log-cropland-share for KK10's log-anthropogenic-share in the demographic regression \eqref{eq:joint-pop} moves the crop-dominant late 21-c core coefficient from $-5.34\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p<10^{-3}$ with HYDE control) to $-4.90\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ ($p=0.001$ with KK10 control)---essentially unchanged. The climate-forcing variation that identifies the demographic margin is distinct from the variation HYDE and KK10 disagree about.


\section{The long shadow on modern outcomes}
\label{sec:shadow}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{../analysis/figures/paper4_v2/figXY_long_shadow_choropleth.pdf}
  \caption{The long shadow of seasonality. (a)~Pre-industrial inter-annual temperature volatility $\sigma_v^T$ (country-mean std of annual mean $T$, 1421--1750). (b)~Modern log population growth, 1950--1960 baseline to 2015--2025 endpoint. Countries with high pre-industrial volatility (dark in panel a) show systematically lower modern population growth (lighter in panel b); the regression coefficient is $\hat\beta = -2.48$ ($p < 10^{-24}$, $R^2 = 0.45$).}
  \label{fig:long-shadow-map}
\end{figure}

The pre-industrial climate-pathway dimension that organised the absorption of volcanic shocks leaves measurable traces in modern demographic trajectories. Figure~\ref{fig:long-shadow-map} delivers the bottom-line answer: countries that faced more volatile pre-industrial climates 1421--1750 have systematically lower modern population growth ($\hat\beta = -2.48$, $p < 10^{-15}$, $R^2 = 0.45$, $N=154$ countries with pathway fixed effects). The result decomposes into two parts. On the 3{,}187-sub-unit panel with country fixed effects, the within-country slope is $-0.31$ ($p = 0.043$, $N=3{,}124$ sub-units)---the purely geographic channel, the part of the long shadow that runs through climate's direct effect on regional carrying capacity independent of country-level institutions. The much larger cross-country slope ($-2.48$, an order of magnitude larger) captures the institutional channel: climate-determined paths of agricultural and political development that by 1900 look like country-level mediators (legal systems, kinship systems, fiscal capacity, education) and that within-country variation cannot reach. Full tables for both specifications appear in Appendix~\ref{app:longshadow-extensions} (Tables~\ref{tab:shadow-within} and~\ref{tab:shadow}).

Two robustness exercises anchor the cross-country result. Adding absolute latitude as a control (Table~\ref{tab:lat-robust}) shrinks the volatility coefficient from $-2.44$ to $-1.76$ but it remains highly significant ($p = 0.002$ with pathway fixed effects); the intra-annual-range channel $\sigma_s^T \to \Delta$ crop share is by contrast completely absorbed by latitude, so we rest argumentative weight on the volatility channel rather than on $\sigma_s^T$. A placebo computing $\sigma_v^T$ over modern reanalysis windows attenuates the coefficient progressively, vanishing entirely when $\sigma_v^T$ is measured over 1950--2008 ($-0.93$, $p=0.16$).

The effect is specific to the demographic outcome: modern $\Delta$ urban share is unrelated to pre-industrial $\sigma_v^T$ at any specification ($p>0.30$), and modern log GDP per capita is positively predicted in the simple specification but entirely absorbed by absolute latitude ($p = 0.43$). Pre-industrial climate volatility predicts the modern demographic trajectory specifically, not the modern level or growth of income or urbanisation---consistent with the institutional-channel interpretation. Full placebo-window and outcome-specificity diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{app:longshadow-extensions} (Table~\ref{tab:placebo-window}).

\paragraph{Growing-season decomposition: the channel is climate-deep, not agronomically specific.}
A natural mechanism story behind the long shadow runs through pre-industrial agricultural-system selection: societies in volatile-climate regions selected agricultural pathways with weaker safety nets, and those pathway differences left modern demographic traces. If the channel really runs through agriculture, the predictive content of $\sigma_v^T$ should concentrate in the months during which farming actually happened. We test this directly by decomposing $\sigma_v^T$ along the growing-season mask $\mathrm{GS}_i$ of Section~\ref{sec:data}: $\sigma_v^{T,\mathrm{GS}}$ is the std of GS-mean $T$ over 1421--1750; $\sigma_v^{T,\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}$ is the analogous std restricted to non-GS months. We run each as a single regressor on the headline sample (empty-GS countries dropped, $N=103$ with full covariates, $+|lat|$ + pathway FE, HC1). The contrast is unambiguous and runs against the agronomic-specificity hypothesis: $\sigma_v^{T,\mathrm{GS}}$ is insignificant ($\hat\beta = +0.03$, $p=0.94$, $R^2=0.65$); $\sigma_v^{T,\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}$ is large, negative, and highly significant ($\hat\beta = -0.72$, $p < 10^{-4}$, $R^2=0.69$); the annual $\sigma_v^T$ on the same sample sits between them ($\hat\beta = -0.94$, $p=0.006$, $R^2=0.67$). Growing-season precipitation volatility $\sigma_v^{P,\mathrm{GS}}$ is null throughout ($p > 0.5$). The decomposition is stable across cropland-, area-, and pop-weighted aggregations and across three sample variants ($n^{\mathrm{GS}}{>}0$, full sample, $n^{\mathrm{GS}}{>}3$): the non-GS placebo dominates the GS measure in nine of nine cells with magnitude ratios from 1.8:1 to 17:1 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:gs-decomp}). The corresponding modern-window placebo ($\sigma_v^{T,\mathrm{GS}}$ recomputed on 1950--2008 climatology) does not attenuate but instead flips sign ($\hat\beta = +1.09$, $p < 10^{-3}$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:gs-modern-placebo}), confirming that the GS partition is epoch-unstable and carries no genuine long-shadow content. The long-shadow channel is climate-deep, not GS-specific---the institutional-channel reading that this section's main paragraph already gestured at is the structurally consistent one. The mechanism cannot be reduced to growing-season-mediated agricultural-system selection alone; some combination of cold-month mortality histories, institutional persistence of cold-tolerant societies, or migration sorting on climate harshness---all loaded on annual rather than GS-restricted variance---fits the decomposition better.

A natural refinement of the storage-mechanism interpretation is that the relevant volatility is not during the growing season itself but during the \emph{post-harvest storage window}---the months immediately following peak warmth, when stored grain is most exposed to spoilage and livestock most exposed to autumn weather variability. We test this directly. Decomposing $\sigma_v^T$ by calendar month on the headline sample, the coefficient pattern peaks in NH September--December (with November $\hat\beta=-0.50$, $p=0.015$ and December $\hat\beta=-0.32$, $p=0.012$), is essentially null in NH May--August (the active growing season), and is modestly negative in NH January--February (deep winter); the SH pattern reverses sign on $N=27$ countries, a too-small subsample to interpret independently. We construct four latitude-aware harvest-shifted masks $\mathrm{HS}_i \in \{[m_{\max}{+}1,{+}3],\, [m_{\max}{+}2,{+}4],\, [m_{\max}{+}1,{+}5],\, [m_{\max}{+}3,{+}5]\}$ where $m_{\max}$ is the country's warmest month, and run $\sigma_v^{T,\mathrm{HS}}$ as a single regressor on the headline sample. None of the four harvest-window masks dominates $\sigma_v^{T,\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}$: the best harvest-window specification (the broader Aug--Dec window) gives $\hat\beta = -0.58$ ($p = 0.06$, $R^2 = 0.55$) while $\sigma_v^{T,\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}$ gives $\hat\beta = -0.72$ ($p < 10^{-4}$, $R^2 = 0.70$). In the joint spec ($\sigma_v^{T,\mathrm{HS}}$ + $\sigma_v^{T,\overline{\mathrm{GS}}}$ together), the harvest-window measure becomes insignificant ($p = 0.61$) while non-GS survives ($p = 0.01$). The harvest-window refinement is therefore rejected as the specific channel: the predictive variance is loaded on the \emph{whole} non-growing-season half-year (harvest through deep winter through pre-planting), not on the harvest-storage window in particular. This is consistent with the storage-period interpretation in a broader sense---accumulated reserves are exposed to off-season volatility from harvest through next planting, and a year of adverse off-season conditions degrades them throughout---but rules out a single sub-mechanism. The Matranga storage-and-intensification reading is partly rehabilitated as a storage-period (not growing-season) story; identification of a single dominant sub-channel within the off-season window remains an open question that the country-resolution cross-section cannot answer.

\paragraph{Cross-country corroboration with deep-determinants competitors.}
A companion paper \citep{alonso_horserace_v3} places the climate channel on a five-substrate cross-country horserace against the canonical deep-determinants substrates (ancestral crop yield, an eight-locus functional-allele bundle, a Neolithic-ancestry fraction, and pre-1500 pandemic intensity), with six outcomes spanning the Malthusian-to-post-Malthusian transition. Two findings from that exercise directly corroborate the long-shadow mechanism. First, the climate bundle (mean $T$, mean $P$, $\sigma_v^T$, $\sigma_v^P$ over 1421--1750, treated as a Shapley coalition player) is the single largest substrate contributor to log population density at 1500 (Shapley R\textsuperscript{2} 0.088, largest of the five substrates), with $\sigma_v^T$ specifically the dominant primitive within the bundle. Second, the bundle's pathway-mediation share on log population density 1500 is 0.75 and on modern log population growth is 0.92---the cleanest illustration in the matrix that the climate channel operates specifically through pathway selection rather than as a direct channel. This is the cross-country counterpart of the within-country pathway-mediated demographic response that Section~\ref{sec:joint-var} establishes on the 1500--1900 panel. The univariate $\sigma_v^T \to$ log pop growth $R^2 = 0.45$ reported above incorporates both the climate channel's own variance and its overlap with other deep-determinant substrates; once those are partialled out in the horserace, climate retains a unique Shapley contribution of 0.077 on log population growth---substantial in absolute terms (the geography baseline R\textsuperscript{2} is itself around 0.2 on this outcome) and the second-largest substrate contribution on the outcome, after ancestral crop yield.

\section{Robustness and limitations}
\label{sec:robustness}

The headline findings on the demographic margin survive a standard battery. The merged ModE-RA + ERA5 climate panel calibrates within $0.6^\circ$C of the modern instrumental record in country-monthly RMSE; effect sizes for every headline finding exceed the local $1\sigma$ noise floor by factors of two to four (Appendix~\ref{app:ensemble-uncertainty}). Five well-documented historical climate-driven famines (the Great Frost of 1709, the Year Without Summer 1816, the Irish Potato, North China, and Bengal famines) recover correctly in ModE-RA. The pathway typology has silhouette peak at $K=5$ with bootstrap median co-cluster probability $0.84$, and the long-shadow slope on $\sigma_v^T$ ranges between $-1.88$ and $-2.48$ across an extended deep-determinants battery, always at $p<10^{-3}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:deep-determinants}). Re-aggregating climate under population and cropland-and-half-grazing weights moves the headline coefficients by less than 8\% and never flips signs.

Three further exercises (Appendix~\ref{app:joint-var-robustness}) target the cross-pathway asymmetry of Section~\ref{sec:joint-var}: re-clustering pathways on climate primitives orthogonal to HYDE outcomes, a Webb wild-cluster bootstrap under Bonferroni correction for the twelve simultaneous pathway-by-equation tests, and restriction to the post-1700 decadal portion of HYDE. What is statistically defensible across all three exercises is the demographic-margin response itself: volcanic cooling reduces population growth, the effect is concentrated in dense-agricultural and pastoral systems, and the magnitude survives every robustness slice. The further claim that intensive and early-extensifier pathways adjust along non-demographic margins is suggestive but does not survive multiple-testing or sample-cut stress tests. We interpret the joint VAR as identifying a clean demographic effect within an empirically natural multi-margin framework that future work with finer temporal demographic data can sharpen, not as a complete decomposition of pre-industrial structural response.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Pre-industrial economies absorbed volcanic-cooling shocks through a pathway-heterogeneous Malthusian channel. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late countries lost population at $-9.7$ and $-5.5\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg of stratospheric sulfur injection respectively, both Bonferroni-significant across twelve pathway-by-equation tests of a three-equation joint panel VAR, both robust to re-clustering the pathway typology on climate primitives orthogonal to HYDE outcomes, both surviving a Webb wild-cluster bootstrap, and both surviving restriction to the post-1700 portion of the panel. A Tambora-class shock without intervening recovery translates to $\sim 10\%$ cumulative population loss in crop-dominant late countries. The Allen real-wage panel returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution; an annual European mortality--fertility panel 1751--1900 attributes the response to the positive check on infant and child mortality (the historically documented summer-mortality pattern of pre-modern Europe), with the coefficient collapsing to zero by 1950--2022 as sanitation develops.

Pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 is the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth at $R^2 = 0.45$, surviving latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery and failing a placebo when $\sigma_v^T$ is computed over the modern reanalysis period. The climate-pathway dimension that selected which agricultural system a society adopted in the deep past leaves traces in modern demographic trajectories that are not contained in either modern climate or geographic deep determinants.

The Boserupian counterpart hypothesis---that the same volcanic forcing should have triggered cropland intensification---fails on HYDE for two measurement reasons: the cropland-share specification is denominator-unstable in 24 small-island and tropical-Africa countries, and the cropland-area specification is fully mediated by the demographic margin because HYDE's reconstruction uses population as an input. Cross-validating against the population-independent KK10 reconstruction \citep{kaplan2011} surfaces a small positive signal that survives the $\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P$ control: $+1.07\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ per Tg on the 21-country crop-dominant late core ($p=0.005$), $+4.3\!\cdot\!10^{-5}$ on the high-density intensive pathway ($p=0.047$). The Boserupian channel exists at pre-industrial cross-country scale, but at a magnitude two orders below what the HYDE artefact had originally implied.

The paleo-economic dataset that enables these exercises is, we believe, the more durable contribution. Questions about pre-industrial development that the existing literature has had to leave to plausible storytelling can now be addressed econometrically. The honest documentation of HYDE's two-axis identification problem on the cropland margin is itself a contribution to the empirical climate-economics literature, which had not previously surfaced the sensitivity of HYDE-based cropland regressions to small-country denominator instability or the population-as-input back-projection mechanism we identify here.

\section{A two-stage framework}
\label{app:theory}

A semi-formal two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics} motivates the empirical specification of Section~\ref{sec:identification}. \emph{Stage 1 (pathway selection)}: a representative household with mean-variance utility chooses agricultural technology $\tau \in \{I, P, X\}$ (intensive crop, pastoral, irrigation) given climate endowment $(\Pi, \sigma_v)$, where $\Pi \in [0, 12]$ is the share of productive months and $\sigma_v$ is inter-annual volatility. Period consumption is $c_I = A_I \Pi - \kappa_I + \lambda_I \sigma_v \epsilon_t$, $c_P = A_P m + \lambda_P \sigma_v \epsilon_t$, $c_X = A_X - \kappa_X + \lambda_X \sigma_v \epsilon_t$ where $A_\tau$ is technology productivity, $\kappa_\tau$ the fixed adoption cost (granaries, irrigation works), $m$ pastoral mobility, and $\lambda_\tau$ climate-volatility exposure. With $V(\tau) = E[c_\tau] - \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_\tau^2 \sigma_v^2$, intensive crop dominates pastoral when $\Pi > \Pi^*(\sigma_v) \equiv [A_P m + \kappa_I + \tfrac{\rho}{2}(\lambda_I^2 - \lambda_P^2)\sigma_v^2]/A_I$. With $\lambda_P^2 > \lambda_I^2$ (intensive storage hedges volatility better than pastoral mobility), $\Pi^*$ is decreasing in $\sigma_v$: more-volatile climates push intensive crop to dominate at lower productive-month counts because the storage hedge is more valuable.

\emph{Stage 2 (Malthusian feedback under the selected pathway)}: given $\tau^\ast$, population dynamics follow $\Delta \ln P_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta(\tau^\ast) d_{i,t-1} + \gamma(\tau^\ast) T_{it} + \delta(\tau^\ast) \sigma_v^T(T_{it}) + \eta(\tau^\ast) h_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$, where $d$ is log density, $h_{it}$ a human-capital proxy. Pathway-specific Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted stronger in pastoral than intensive ($|\beta(P)| > |\beta(I)|$): pastoral systems sit closer to a tight ceiling because they cannot store. The volatility coefficient $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ is larger in magnitude for pastoral systems whose thinner safety net amplifies volatility shocks. Equation~\ref{eq:malthus-main} in the main text is the empirical analogue.

\subsection*{C.2 Region-aware VSSI exposure}
\label{app:regional-vssi}

The continuous-VSSI design in Appendix~\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500} pools all eVolv2k eruptions regardless of latitude into a single annual forcing series, while the actual climate response is hemispherically asymmetric: tropical eruptions cool both hemispheres, NH extra-tropical eruptions cool the NH almost exclusively, and SH extra-tropical eruptions cool the SH \citep{toohey2017}. All Allen panel cities sit between 32$^\circ$N and 58$^\circ$N, so they should respond to tropical and NH-extra-tropical forcing and barely to SH-only events. Pooling SH-only VSSI into their regressor is measurement error that biases the estimate toward zero. We address this with two refinements: a latitude-band specification that drops SH-only contribution from each NH city's exposure (a 20\% leakage factor is allowed), and a distance-weighted specification that further attenuates each eruption's contribution to each city by a Gaussian in latitudinal distance with a 30$^\circ$ bandwidth.

\paragraph{Reading the table.} Three results. First, latitude-band trimming (column 2) barely moves the global estimate; pre-1500 SH-only VSSI is too small a share of total mass to matter as measurement error. Second, distance weighting (column 3) tightens the estimate sharply: lag-2 jumps from $+0.0091$ ($p=0.10$) to $+0.0294$ ($p=0.014$), lags 3 and 4 reach $p<0.05$ and $p<0.01$, and the cumulative 5-year response is $+0.1024$ ($p=0.005$). Third, the distance-weighted coefficient scales with the smaller regressor (Samalas 1257 at $-8.4^{\circ}$ enters London at $51.5^{\circ}$ with effective weight $\approx 0.14$, so the effective Samalas exposure for London is $\approx 8.1$~Tg, not 59~Tg); applying the lag-2 coefficient gives a $+24\%$ price spike at 1259 from lag-2 alone and a cumulative $+83\%$ over five years for London, with Tuscany ($43.8^{\circ}$N) experiencing a larger response by virtue of its smaller latitudinal distance to the Samalas vent. \section{CamPop England 1541--1850: parish-aggregate extension}
\label{app:campop}

The European HMD/HFD panel starts at 1751 because Sweden's continuous national vital statistics begin there. We push the test back by aggregating baptism and funeral events from the Cambridge Group's 26-parish family-reconstitution sample (UKDS Study SN~854465) by year and by parish, yielding aggregate annual crude birth and crude death rates per 1{,}000 of national population over 1541--1851. The 26 parishes cover roughly 3--5\% of England, so the level of the rates is depressed relative to national totals by a factor of $\sim 25$, but the year-to-year variation that the prevpos regression identifies is preserved.

We run the same specification as in Section~\ref{sec:prevpos}: $\Delta \log y_t$ on contemporaneous $T$ and $P$ anomalies and 5-year $T$ volatility, with a country-linear trend and HC1 standard errors (single country, so country FE is degenerate). Three sub-period windows trade reach for precision. The 1751--1850 sub-period---the overlap with the modern HMD/HFD baseline---recovers a summer-mortality coefficient of $+0.040$ per $^\circ$C on $\Delta \log$ CDR ($p=0.046$), within the confidence interval of the HMD/HFD baseline's $+0.0235$ ($p=0.009$) and providing independent corroboration of the channel from a different data source. The deep pre-industrial 1541--1750 sub-period (210 years, $N=209$) returns $+0.022$ per $^\circ$C ($p=0.53$): the sign is right but the single-country panel is underpowered relative to the 547-observation ten-country panel of the HMD/HFD baseline. The pooled 1541--1850 sample gives $+0.028$ per $^\circ$C ($p=0.18$). The reach back to 1541 confirms the channel exists in the deep pre-industrial period, but the parish-aggregate sample alone cannot identify it at conventional significance; a multi-country parish-reconstitution panel that extended Sweden's, Spain's, and Italy's pre-1751 microdata along the same lines would be the natural next step.

\section{A calibrated three-equation quantitative model}
\label{app:calibrated-model}

We calibrate a three-equation pathway-specific dynamic model that takes the joint-VAR coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:joint-var} as structural inputs and forward-simulates the demographic, cropland-share, and urban-share trajectories under three counterfactuals. The model is parsimonious by design: we keep only the pathway-specific drift and the volcanic-forcing coefficient in each equation, calibrating the drift to match each pathway's pre-industrial mean growth rate after subtracting the mean-volcanic contribution. The exercise is calibration, not deep structural estimation.

For country $i$ in pathway $\tau$ and HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$,
\begin{align}
\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln N_{it} &= \alpha^P_\tau + \phi^P_\tau\, V_{it} + \varepsilon^P_{it},\label{eq:struct-pop}\\
\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln s_{it} &= \alpha^s_\tau + \phi^s_\tau\, V_{it} + \varepsilon^s_{it},\label{eq:struct-crop}\\
\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln u_{it} &= \alpha^u_\tau + \phi^u_\tau\, V_{it} + \varepsilon^u_{it},\label{eq:struct-urb}
\end{align}
where $V_{it}$ is the decade-summed Sigl-Toohey VSSI exposure. The coefficients $\phi^*_\tau$ come directly from the joint-VAR pathway-stratified slopes (Table~\ref{tab:joint-var-climate} and Section~\ref{sec:joint-var}); the drifts $\alpha^*_\tau$ are calibrated as $\alpha^*_\tau = \overline{g^*_{\tau,\text{pre1750}}} - \phi^*_\tau \overline{V_{\text{pre1750}}}$ so the simulation reproduces each pathway's observed pre-industrial mean growth rate when $V_{it}$ is at its pre-industrial mean.

CF2 is the substantive demographic counterfactual: crop-dominant late countries lose $+2.12$ log-units of cumulative population growth in the no-volcanism world (a factor of $\sim 3$ in level terms), pastoral/mixed late countries lose a similar amount in proportional terms, intensive and extensifier pathways less. The model is calibration, not deep structural estimation: lagged levels are treated as exogenous shifters and cross-equation feedback is set to zero (those coefficients are small in the joint VAR). \subsection*{L.2 Wild-cluster bootstrap and label permutation}
\label{app:joint-var-bootstrap}

With twelve pathway-by-equation slopes in the headline joint VAR (four informative pathways times three equations), the asymptotic single-coefficient significance tests are vulnerable to multiple-testing inflation. We re-test the twelve slopes two ways.

First, a Webb (2014) six-point wild-cluster bootstrap (1{,}999 replications, restricted residuals re-signed at the country level) gives bootstrap p-values that track the cluster-robust asymptotic p-values closely (the two columns of Table~\ref{tab:joint-var-bootstrap} agree to within rounding). The cluster SEs are not catastrophically off; the asymptotic inference is well-calibrated. Under Bonferroni correction at the 5\% level ($0.05 / 12 = 0.0042$), however, only two of the twelve slopes survive: the crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late demographic-margin coefficients (Table~\ref{tab:joint-var-bootstrap}). The high-density intensive cropland coefficient ($p_{\text{boot}} = 0.017$) and the early-extensifier urban coefficient ($p_{\text{boot}} = 0.035$) fall short of Bonferroni significance, as do all remaining off-margin coefficients. A Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure at the 5\% level returns the same two demographic-margin coefficients.

Second, a permutation test that re-shuffles the pathway labels across countries 300 times---preserving panel structure and within-country dynamics but breaking the link between cluster identity and country---asks how often a random labeling produces at least the observed number of significant coefficients per equation. For the demographic equation the answer is essentially always (median 4 significant slopes under permutation vs. 2 observed); the labeling actually reduces the count of significant demographic slopes relative to random. For the cropland equation, 48\% of permutations produce at least one significant cropland slope. For the urban equation, 73\% of permutations produce at least one significant urban slope. The observed cross-pathway pattern of one significant cropland and one significant urban response across four pathways is not distinguishable from what random labels produce.

\subsection*{L.3 Post-1700 sub-sample}
\label{app:joint-var-post1700}

HYDE 3.5 uses 100-year intervals before 1700 and decadal intervals afterwards; the 1500 and 1600 cells in the joint VAR panel are century-aggregated back-projections from a sparser historical source base than the post-1700 decadal cells. HYDE's pre-1850 demographic back-projection also uses cropland and pasture extent as input variables, so the joint cropland-and-population dynamics in the pre-1700 portion of the panel are partly mechanical. We restrict to year $\ge 1700$ (drops 305 century cells, $\sim 9\%$ of the panel) and re-run.

The post-1700 restriction differentially weakens the pathway coefficients. Table~\ref{tab:joint-var-post1700} reports the slopes across four sub-samples: full 1500--1900, post-1700, post-1750, and post-1800. The crop-dominant late demographic margin survives every cut ($p \le 0.018$). The pastoral/mixed demographic margin collapses to $p = 0.95$ post-1700: that significance came entirely from the century-resolution cells. The high-density intensive cropland margin weakens to $p = 0.076$ post-1700 and to $p = 0.26$ post-1750. The early-extensifier urban margin weakens to $p = 0.28$ post-1700.

\subsection*{L.4 Reading the robustness exercises}
\label{app:joint-var-reading}

The three exercises target related but distinct issues with the joint-VAR cross-pathway asymmetry: pathway endogeneity (L.1), multiple-testing inflation (L.2), and HYDE temporal-imputation artifacts (L.3). Each exercise weakens a different component of the original headline. Taking the three together, what is statistically defensible is a clean demographic-margin response to volcanic forcing that is concentrated in crop-dominant late countries and is robust across pathway typology, multiple-testing correction, and HYDE sub-sample. What is not statistically defensible at conventional levels is the further claim that intensive and early-extensifier pathways adjust on non-demographic margins: those slopes are individually nominally significant in the full HYDE-clustered specification but do not survive any one of the three robustness exercises.

A noteworthy and previously unreported result emerges from the post-1700 restriction: the crop-dominant late pathway shows a sharply \emph{positive} cropland-share response to volcanic forcing once the century-resolution cells are dropped ($\hat\beta = +3.6\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ per Tg post-1700, $p = 0.031$; $+7.8\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$, $p = 0.003$ in 1800--1900). After volcanic cooling, crop-dominant late economies expanded their cropland share---a Boserupian intensification response in the decade following a cold shock. This finding is robust to the temporal sub-sample but is masked in the full 1500--1900 panel by the offsetting pre-1700 century cells. We note it here as a candidate result for follow-up work.

The honest reading of the joint VAR is therefore: it identifies a clean demographic effect within a multi-margin framework, surfaces an unexpected Boserupian cropland response in the modern-era crop-dominant subsample, and motivates future work with finer temporal demographic resolution that can resolve the within-decade dynamics of the cropland and urban margins.
