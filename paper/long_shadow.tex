\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We ask whether the climate endowment that shaped where humans first farmed also shaped where they first escaped the Malthusian trap. To answer it we build a new monthly paleo-economic panel for 196 countries and 3{,}187 sub-national units reaching back to 1421~CE, assembled by aggregating the ModE-RA paleo-reanalysis onto HYDE 3.5 land-use cells and linking the resulting climate panel to the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k volcanic record, the Brecke conflict catalogue, and the Allen-Nuffield wage-and-price archive. Within-country regressions identify a Malthusian density coefficient that weakens monotonically and vanishes by 1900, and a climate-volatility coefficient that flips from helpful to strongly harmful as twentieth-century monoculture replaces mixed pre-industrial systems. Continuous volcanic forcing from the Sigl-Toohey ice-core record 1500--1900 produces a sharply pathway-heterogeneous demographic response, and a region-aware extension of the volcanic test back to the 1257 Samalas eruption recovers a supply-side wheat-price spike at lag two years that is robust to conflict and pandemic controls. A three-equation panel VAR of population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth then reveals a clean cross-pathway asymmetry of preferred adjustment margins: traditional crop-and-pastoral systems absorb climate shocks demographically, intensive systems rebalance cropland-versus-grazing composition, and the early-extensifier pathway absorbs by slowing urbanization---the unified-growth-theory exit margin. An annual European panel of age-specific mortality and fertility 1751--1900 decomposes the demographic response into preventive and positive Malthusian checks and finds it is dominated by the positive check on infant and child mortality, the historically documented summer-mortality pattern. An Allen real-wage panel for six European countries 1421--1850 returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution, and pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 emerges as the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth ($R^2 = 0.45$), surviving both latitude and deep-determinant controls. Seasonality, properly measured, casts a long shadow over development.

\section{Introduction}

For most of human history, productivity gains were absorbed by population growth. Through ten millennia of agriculture, living standards drifted within a narrow band; the technological frontier advanced, but Malthusian feedback returned populations to subsistence. Then, beginning in eighteenth-century northwestern Europe and propagating outward over the next two centuries, the trap broke. Income per capita rose without provoking compensating fertility, and the modern era of sustained growth began. The leading question of unified growth theory \citep{galor2011unified} is \emph{why}---what allowed some societies to break the feedback while others remained inside it---and the modern empirical literature on the question has produced a remarkable convergence on a short list of candidate answers: deep human-capital accumulation \citep{galor2000population}, a peculiar Western demographic regime built on late marriage and high mortality from urban disease \citep{voigtlander2013how}, gradual selection on individual industriousness in the long pre-industrial period \citep{clark2007farewell}, the diffusion of New World crops and the consequent relaxation of the Malthusian ceiling \citep{nunn2011potato}, and the institutional inheritances that allowed productivity gains to translate into welfare rather than headcount \citep{ashraf2013out, galor2002natural}.

Each of these stories has a climate sub-text. \citet{matranga2024} has recently argued that the original agricultural transition itself was a climate event: a sharpening of intra-annual variability at the end of the Pleistocene raised the value of storable surplus and tipped the optimal subsistence calculus from foraging to settled cultivation. \citet{ashraf2011dynamics} demonstrate that the strength of the Malthusian feedback can be estimated directly from pre-industrial cross-country panels and that the variation it identifies is rich enough to discriminate between models, but in their setting climate forcing is a residual term inside the country fixed effect, not a measurable regressor. \citet{dell2012temperature, burke2015global} establish that temperature does have first-order effects on economic activity in the modern instrumental record, but the modern panel is short and post-industrial; the relevant question for unified growth theory is what climate forcing did during the centuries when Malthusian feedback was active. \citet{hsiang2017global} integrates these damages economy-wide; \citet{auffhammer2011weather, deschenes2007economic} provide careful agricultural microfoundations. None of these papers reaches the empirical setting that matters most for the Malthusian-trap question: a multi-century panel of pre-industrial populations linked to a multi-century panel of monthly climate forcing.

The reason is data. Pre-industrial population and land use have been mapped at the country or continent level by historical demographers for decades but only recently at finer resolution; the construction of HYDE~3.5 \citep{goldewijk2017}, with population and land-use estimates for 197 countries from 10{,}000~BCE to 2025~CE at 5-arcminute resolution and across three uncertainty scenarios, makes the spatial side workable. Climate has been harder. The PAGES~2k continental temperature reconstructions \citep{pages2k2019} have organised the proxy record at decadal-to-centennial resolution and continental geography, sufficient for global narratives but not for what a Strasbourg wheat harvest looked like in 1437. The breakthrough that we exploit here is ModE-RA \citep{franke2023modera}, a monthly global paleo-reanalysis covering 1421--2008~CE that assimilates documentary records, tree rings, ice cores, corals, and historical instruments into a coupled numerical model, returning gridded monthly temperature and precipitation fields. Combined with the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k ice-core record of volcanic stratospheric sulfur injection over the past 2{,}500 years \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017}, the Brecke catalogue of all violent conflicts since 1400 \citep{brecke1999}, and the Allen-Nuffield archive of city-level wages and prices since 1259 \citep{allen2001divergence}, ModE-RA delivers, for the first time, the regressors needed to run pre-industrial Malthusian econometrics with real annual climate forcing rather than assuming the forcing into a fixed effect.

What we do with the resulting paleo-economic panel is the empirical core of the paper. The argument we test is that the structure of climate---specifically, the share of calendar months that simultaneously deliver enough warmth and enough water for rain-fed cropping---selected which agricultural system a society adopted in the deep past, and that the selected system in turn determined the strength of the Malthusian feedback, the demographic response to year-to-year climate shocks, and the timing of escape from the trap. We sketch a two-stage model that gives the argument formal content (full statement in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}) and use the appendix model to motivate the within-country panel regression of population growth on lagged density, temperature and precipitation anomalies, and inter-annual temperature volatility that anchors the empirical work. The same regression is then used to test corroborating pieces of evidence: the demographic response to volcanic cooling, the welfare-side response of real wages to lagged population, and the long-shadow projection of pre-industrial climate volatility onto modern population growth across countries.

Our contribution is not primarily theoretical---the intellectual frame is Galor's, the climate mechanism is Matranga's. It is empirical and methodological. We build a new multi-source paleo-economic panel that no prior paper has assembled. We run within-country Malthusian regressions with real annual climate forcing for the entire 1421--1950 sample, not just for the modern instrumental overlap. We exploit HYDE's sub-national grid to identify the storage-demand-to-pathway link off purely within-country variation, sidestepping the country-level confounders that have shadowed the cross-country growth literature \citep{durlauf2005}. We extend the volcanic test back to the 1257 Samalas eruption using city-level wheat prices and a region-aware exposure measure that follows the Toohey-Sigl latitudinal-band convention. And we explicitly assess robustness against the most natural pre-industrial confound---contemporaneous war and pandemic activity---by linking the climate panel to the Brecke conflict catalogue and a hand-coded plague catalogue and showing that the conflict regressors retain enough within-city, within-decade variation under two-way fixed effects to identify their separate effects, which leaves the volcanic signal essentially unchanged.

Seven findings emerge. Pathway selection is governed by the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that captures storage demand, not by raw temperature seasonality, and the storage-demand effect is identified with effects $3.5\times$ the between-country estimate once country fixed effects are imposed. Within-country Malthusian regressions on 1421--1950 climate forcing surface a within-paper signature of the demographic transition---the density coefficient progressively weakens and vanishes after 1900---and a sign-flip on inter-annual temperature volatility, from helpful in the long pre-industrial period to strongly harmful in 1900--1950, consistent with twentieth-century monoculture replacing mixed pre-industrial systems. Continuous volcanic forcing from Sigl-Toohey 1500--1900 produces a sharply pathway-heterogeneous demographic response; the response survives the pre-1500 extension to the Samalas 1257 eruption when run on annual wheat prices with a region-aware exposure measure and conflict-and-pandemic controls. A joint three-equation panel VAR of population, cropland-share, and urban-share growth recovers a clean cross-pathway asymmetry of preferred adjustment margins that no single-equation regression could surface: crop-dominant and pastoral systems absorb climate shocks demographically, high-density intensive systems rebalance cropland-versus-grazing composition without losing people, and early extensifiers absorb by slowing urbanization---the unified-growth-theory exit margin. An annual European panel of age-specific mortality and fertility 1751--1900, anchored by Sweden's 149-year continuous series, decomposes the Malthusian response into its preventive (fertility) and positive (mortality) checks and shows the response is dominated by the positive check on infant and child mortality. An Allen real-wage panel for six European countries 1421--1850 corroborates the channel on the welfare margin: lagged population reduces real wages, warmer years raise them, and the Malthusian coefficient tightens under a city-buffer climate specification. Pre-industrial inter-annual climate volatility 1421--1750 then emerges as the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth ($R^2 = 0.45$) and survives both latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery.

The paper proceeds in four substantive sections after the introduction. Section~\ref{sec:data} introduces the new monthly paleo-economic panel and the climate-pathway typology it discriminates. Section~\ref{sec:identification} works out the within-country identification strategy that the appendix model motivates and runs the headline 1421--1950 Malthusian regression. Section~\ref{sec:corroboration} corroborates the channel through three triangulating exercises: the volcanic event study (with its pre-XV-century price-side extension), the European real-wage panel, and the three-equation joint VAR that traces the structural-response margins jointly across population, cropland composition, and urbanization. Section~\ref{sec:shadow} runs the long-shadow regression on modern population growth and shows how it survives latitude and deep-determinant controls. Section~\ref{sec:structural} calibrates a parsimonious quantitative model to translate the reduced-form coefficients into counterfactual magnitudes. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} consolidates robustness; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The appendix presents the two-stage theoretical framework, the pre-XV-century volcanic extension, the conflict and pandemic robustness exercises, the region-aware VSSI specifications, and supplementary diagnostics.

\section{A new monthly paleo-economic panel}
\label{sec:data}

The paper rests on a paleo-economic dataset that we assemble for the first time and that does most of the empirical work. We describe its construction, validate its alignment with the modern instrumental record where they overlap, and then use it to characterise the climate-pathway typology that organises the rest of the paper. The dataset itself is, we believe, the more durable contribution: it can support econometric questions about pre-industrial development that the existing climate-history literature has been unable to address.

\subsection{Construction}

The dataset has three substantive layers and three event layers, all merged onto a common country-year (or city-year, or sub-unit-year) grid. The first substantive layer is HYDE~3.5 \citep{goldewijk2017}, which provides gridded estimates of population, cropland, grazing land, irrigated land, rice cultivation, and urban population at 5-arcminute resolution from 10{,}000~BCE to 2025~CE under three uncertainty scenarios. We aggregate to country totals using the \texttt{iso\_cr} country raster and to 3{,}187 sub-national units using the \texttt{sub\_iso\_cr} raster. The HYDE temporal grid is irregular: 100-year intervals before 1700, decadal 1700--1950, and annual thereafter. The pre-industrial decadal sample is what powers most of the regressions; we use the century-spaced pre-1500 sample only in the appendix extension.

The second layer is ModE-RA \citep{franke2023modera}, a monthly global paleo-reanalysis covering 1421--2008~CE that assimilates documentary records, tree rings, ice cores, corals, and historical instruments into a coupled numerical-model background, returning monthly temperature and precipitation anomalies relative to the 1901--2000 mean on a $192 \times 96$ Gaussian grid. We aggregate each ModE-RA cell onto HYDE country and sub-national units via cosine-latitude-weighted area sums on the 5-arcminute country raster; the CRU~TS~4.09 \citep{harris2020cruts} 1901--1950 country-month climatology, computed analogously, is added back to the anomalies to obtain absolute monthly temperatures and precipitation throughout 1421--2008. We rebuild ERA5 \citep{hersbach2020era5} at the country level by area-weighting the raw $0.25^\circ$ ERA5 monthly fields onto HYDE country boundaries; per-country mean bias is removed by a constant offset estimated in the 1950--2008 overlap with ModE-RA.

Three derived climate measures play headline roles in what follows. The intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T(i,t) = \max_m T_{i,t,m} - \min_m T_{i,t,m}$ captures within-year variation. The productive-months index $\mathrm{Prod}_{i,t}$ counts calendar months in which $5^\circ\mathrm{C} \le T_{i,t,m} \le 30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $P_{i,t,m} \ge 30~\mathrm{mm}$---months simultaneously warm enough and wet enough for rain-fed cropping; the complement $12 - \mathrm{Prod}_{i,t}$ is the non-productive-months count and captures storage demand. The inter-annual temperature volatility $\sigma_v^T(i,t)$ is the country-specific standard deviation of annual mean temperature in a 30-year centred window. In within-interval panel regressions we use within-interval volatility rather than the rolling window. We report a published-criterion alternative based on FAO-style wheat suitability in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

The three event layers attach to specific (country, year) or (city, year) cells of the panel. The Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k v4 ice-core record \citep{sigl2024evolv2k, toohey2017} provides annual estimates of global stratospheric volcanic sulfur injection from 500~BCE to 1900~CE, with eruption latitudes for each event; we link this to the climate panel as both a discrete event-study indicator and a continuous distributed-lag regressor. The Brecke Conflict Catalogue \citep{brecke1999} adds 3{,}708 violent conflicts 1400--1999 with start- and end-years and free-text actor descriptions, plus 1{,}147 European conflicts 900--1402 in a supplementary extension. We string-match the actor descriptions to nine medieval regions to produce binary war indicators per (city, year), and hand-code 30 sieges of specific panel cities and 49 plague episodes from standard catalogues. The Allen-Nuffield wage-and-price archive \citep{allen2001divergence} contributes silver-content-normalised wheat prices for 17 European cities 1259--1914 and real-wage series for 8 cities 1264--1850; we parse the silver-grams-per-hectoliter wheat-price column directly from each city's xls file and augment with Tuscan prices from \citet{malanima2002} and the Roman-Egypt wheat-price series of \citet{harper2016people}.

\subsection{Validation}

The country-level monthly climate panel matches the modern instrumental record where they overlap. In the 1950--2008 ModE-RA/ERA5 overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}), the median country-month mean bias is $0.61^\circ$C, the median country-monthly $T$ correlation is $0.971$ (77.6\% of countries exceed $r=0.90$), and the median country-annual anomaly correlation is $0.938$ after detrending. The agreement is strong enough that we treat ModE-RA $+$ CRU climatology as a substantive measurement of country-monthly climate rather than as a stand-in. ModE-RA's ensemble $1\sigma$ uncertainty in country-month temperature, propagated through the area-weighted aggregation, is on the order of $0.9^\circ$C in 1421--1500 and falls steadily to $0.37^\circ$C by 1850--1949; ModE-RA precipitation uncertainty traces the same secular decline from $31.6$ to $15.4$~mm/month. Past 1950, we splice in our country-level ERA5 rebuild, which the bias-correction step described above brings onto the same scale as the paleo-reanalysis. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble} reports the merged-panel uncertainty across 1421--2025, including the ERA5--ModE-RA monthly RMSE in the 1950--2008 overlap as a cross-validation diagnostic. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} discusses the implications for the headline regressions.

\subsection{Five agricultural pathways}
\label{sec:pathways}

The new panel discriminates a typology of agricultural development that the existing literature has discussed informally but not measured systematically. Applying $K$-means clustering to standardised HYDE trajectory features---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, 1000~CE, and 0~CE, crop share at 1750, rice share at 1750, irrigation share at 1750, urban share at 1750, two interval population-growth rates, and an agricultural-intensity change index---returns a five-cluster partition. The choice of $K=5$ is the silhouette local maximum on this feature set (0.40 at $K=5$, with a sharp drop to 0.23 at $K=6$); bootstrap stability of the partition is 0.84 in median country-pair co-clustering probability across 200 resamples. The five pathways are high-density intensive (Japan, India, France, Germany, Netherlands, 16 countries), early extensifiers (Britain, Switzerland, Austria, Denmark, Israel, 29 countries), crop-dominant late (Spain, Poland, Portugal, Thailand, 46 countries), pastoral/mixed late (China, Nigeria, Brazil, Iran, Mexico, 65 countries), and the irrigation pioneer Egypt as a singleton (Table~\ref{tab:pathways}). The pathways differ along all five clustering dimensions---peak agricultural expansion year, density at 1750, crop share, irrigation share, and rate of demographic expansion---in ways that match the historical literature's informal taxonomy.

\subsection{Climate signatures of the pathways}
\label{sec:stage1}

The new panel allows the first systematic measurement of pathway-level climate signatures. The natural Matranga measure---raw intra-annual temperature range $\sigma_s^T$ averaged over 1421--1750---does \emph{not} significantly distinguish pathways at the country level. A one-way ANOVA on 153 countries (dropping the singleton Egypt cluster) yields $F=0.50$, $p=0.68$; a multinomial logit on $\sigma_s^T$, mean temperature, and growing degree days returns pseudo-$R^2=0.025$ and LLR $p=0.39$. What does distinguish pathways is the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that determines storage demand. The productive-months index defined above differs sharply across pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:storage}, Table~\ref{tab:storage}):

A one-way ANOVA on productive months returns $F=6.31$, $p<0.001$. A multinomial logit with the productive-months index and the storage index ($(12-\mathrm{Prod})\cdot \sigma_s^T/12$) attains pseudo-$R^2=0.065$ and LLR $p=0.0003$. Productive months reduce the probability of pastoral selection ($\beta=-0.56$, $p<10^{-3}$ relative to the crop-dominant baseline) and storage demand reduces the probability of pastoral selection ($\beta=-0.18$, $p=0.014$). The lone irrigation pioneer (Egypt) sits at the extreme: zero rain-fed productive months but enormous storage demand---consistent with the historical record of state-led Nile irrigation as the institutional response to a climate that could not support rain-fed agriculture.

The Matranga story survives, in other words, but it operates through the \emph{joint} structure of temperature and precipitation rather than through temperature variability alone. Storage demand is not a feature of cold winters by themselves but of the combination of growing-season productivity and the off-season's harshness.

\section{From storage demand to the Malthusian regression}
\label{sec:identification}

The empirical work that follows is organised around the within-country identification strategy that the appendix model motivates. Appendix~\ref{app:theory} sketches a two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics} that delivers two empirical predictions. First, in a society's far past, the share of climatically productive months $\Pi$ jointly with inter-annual climate volatility $\sigma_v$ selects which agricultural system $\tau \in \{$intensive crop, extensive pastoral, irrigation-based$\}$ the society adopts; the model produces a productive-months threshold $\Pi^*(\sigma_v)$ above which intensive crop dominates pastoral (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}, Equation~\ref{eq:threshold}). Second, given the selected pathway, the demographic dynamics follow a Malthusian regression with pathway-specific coefficients on lagged density, level temperature, and inter-annual temperature volatility:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \ln P_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta(\tau^\ast)\, d_{i,t-1} \;+\; \gamma(\tau^\ast)\, T_{it} \;+\; \delta(\tau^\ast)\, \sigma_v^T(T_{it}) \;+\; \eta(\tau^\ast)\, h_{it} \;+\; \varepsilon_{it}.
  \label{eq:malthus-main}
\end{equation}
This is the regression we run. The Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted stronger in pastoral than in intensive systems---pastoral systems sit closer to a tight ceiling because they cannot store, intensive systems carry capital that smooths the feedback---and the volatility coefficient $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted to be larger in magnitude for pastoral systems, whose thinner safety nets do not buffer year-to-year shocks. The level-temperature coefficient $\gamma(\tau^\ast)$ admits both signs depending on whether the baseline climate is at the warm or cool end of the productive range; we treat its empirical sign as informative, not predicted. The human-capital coefficient $\eta(\tau^\ast)$ is the rate at which the Malthusian feedback weakens with secular accumulation of literacy, urbanisation, and capital, and is the channel through which the demographic transition appears in our sub-period regressions.

We take the appendix model as motivational, not literal. The full structural exercise we run later (Section~\ref{sec:structural}) calibrates the model's pathway-specific parameters from the reduced-form coefficients estimated here and uses the calibrated system for counterfactual simulations rather than for inference. Two refinements of the regression specification are forced by the data. First, HYDE's pre-industrial temporal grid is irregular (century pre-1700, decadal 1700--1950), so the dependent variable is annualised log population growth across each HYDE interval rather than annual growth. Second, the headline pathway-stratified estimates use a within-country fixed-effects specification with country-clustered standard errors; we report sub-period stability across 1421--1750, 1750--1900, and 1900--1950 to surface a within-paper signature of the demographic transition.

\subsection{Sub-national identification of the storage-demand channel}
\label{sec:subnational}

Before turning to the Malthusian regression directly, we use the new sub-national panel to identify the first of the model's two channels---climate's selection of agricultural pathway---off purely within-country variation. The country-level Stage 1 regressions in Section~\ref{sec:pathways} cannot rule out unobserved country-level confounders (legal institutions, historical conquest, distance to coast) that could drive both pathway and climate measures spuriously. We sharpen identification by descending to HYDE's 5-arcminute sub-national grid: 3{,}187 sub-units across 196 countries. We aggregate ModE-RA monthly fields onto each sub-unit by the same area-weighting procedure used for countries, compute the pre-industrial productive-months index per sub-unit, and run within-country regressions of HYDE agricultural outcomes on the productive-months index with country fixed effects.

The country fixed effect absorbs every country-level confounder. The remaining variation is purely intra-national. To the extent that the storage-demand mechanism operates through climate \emph{within} a country, this specification identifies it cleanly. Identification fails only under cross-sub-unit confounders that vary within country and that we have failed to control for---a much narrower threat than at country level.

Table~\ref{tab:subnational} reports the within-country regressions of crop share and log population density on productive months at three HYDE timesteps. At 1750, sub-units with one more productive month per year are 6.7~pp higher in crop share ($p=0.019$) and 0.66 log-units higher in log population density ($p<10^{-3}$). The within-country point estimates are 3.5--5 times the between-country estimates, consistent with the between estimates being attenuated by country-level mean reversion. The within pattern is stable through 1850 and 1950.

We read this as the paper's cleanest causal evidence for the storage-demand $\to$ agricultural-system channel. Whatever unobserved country-level factors confound the cross-country regression---and we believe there are many---are differenced out here. Two-way clustering of standard errors by country and 10$^\circ$ latitude band, which accounts for cross-country spatial correlation in climate shocks, tightens rather than loosens inference: the within-country slope's $p$-value falls from $0.019$ under country-only clustering to $0.0001$ under two-way clustering, because the latitude band picks up climate-correlated noise that country clustering alone treated as informative variation.

\subsection{Malthusian dynamics with real annual climate, 1421--1950}
\label{sec:stage2}

Pre-industrial Malthusian regressions in the existing literature \citep{ashraf2011dynamics} have been run without explicit climate controls because suitable climate forcing has not been available at country-year resolution back to the pre-industrial period. The ModE-RA panel makes that possible. For each (country $i$, HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$) in the 1421--1950 sample, we compute the interval-mean temperature anomaly $\bar T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ and precipitation anomaly $\bar P_{i,[t,t+1)}$ relative to the country mean, the within-interval $T$ standard deviation $\sigma^T_{i,[t,t+1)}$ as a measure of inter-annual volatility, and the analogous precipitation volatility. The estimating equation, the empirical analogue of (\ref{eq:malthus-main}), is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,[t,t+1)} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta\, \ln d_{it} \;+\; \gamma_T \bar T \;+\; \gamma_P \bar P \;+\; \delta_T \sigma^T \;+\; \delta_P \sigma^P \;+\; \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:malthus2}
\end{equation}
estimated by within-country fixed effects with country-clustered standard errors.

Pastoral systems show the standard Malthusian sign ($-0.0059$, $p=0.002$). High-density intensive and early extensifier systems have density coefficients between these extremes. Inter-annual $T$ volatility significantly depresses growth in the two crop-based capital-intensive pathways---high-density intensive ($p=0.035$) and early extensifiers ($p=0.031$)---and is insignificant in the pastoral pathway, consistent with the pastoral system's traditional hedge of mobility and herd diversification.

\subsubsection{War and pandemic robustness}
\label{sec:malthus-conflict-robust}

The most natural confound for the climate coefficients in Equation~\eqref{eq:malthus2} is that wars and pandemics correlate in time with climate shocks and have their own direct effects on population. We address this by augmenting the Brecke conflict catalogue \citep{brecke1999} with broad-region plague catalogues (the canonical Black Death wave plus 29 documented European recurrences 1361--1772; the Justinianic plague plus 17 late-antique and medieval recurrences for the Mediterranean region; the Antonine and Cyprian plagues for the Roman period) to build country-year indicators \texttt{war\_active}$_{i,t}$ and \texttt{plague\_active}$_{i,t}$ for all 196 panel countries 1421--1950. The country-year war indicator is built by string-matching the Brecke conflict actor-name field against 196 country-name patterns including standard historical variants (\emph{Burgundy}, \emph{Ottoman}, \emph{Mughal}, \emph{Mongol}, etc.). The HYDE-interval fractions \texttt{war\_frac}$_{i,[t,t+1)}$ and \texttt{plague\_frac}$_{i,[t,t+1)}$ are then added to the right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:malthus2}.

The headline climate coefficients are essentially unchanged. The pooled temperature-anomaly coefficient moves from $+0.0055$ ($p<10^{-9}$) to $+0.0051$ ($p<10^{-9}$); the within-interval temperature-volatility coefficient moves from $+0.0038$ ($p=0.036$) to $+0.0034$ ($p=0.052$). The two control variables enter sensibly: plague-active intervals are associated with annualised log population growth lower by $-0.0056$ ($p=0.020$), in line with documented mortality from major plague waves; the country-decade war indicator is small and insignificant ($-0.0003$, $p=0.75$), reflecting the fact that the binary national-war variable is too coarse to identify war-specific mortality at decadal resolution. The pathway-stratified results echo the pooled picture: each pathway's density and volatility coefficients change by less than 5\% with the controls added; the modern positive-density effect in the early-extensifier pathway ($+0.0066 \to +0.0062$, both $p<10^{-5}$) is robust, and pastoral systems pick up a marginal war coefficient ($-0.0028$, $p=0.051$) consistent with their lower demographic buffers. Full numbers are in Table~\ref{tab:malthus-conflict} below.

\section{Corroboration: volcanic forcing, welfare, and the joint structural response}
\label{sec:corroboration}

The within-country Malthusian regression establishes the headline pattern, but it leaves three natural concerns open. The first is that within-interval variation in temperature is not strictly exogenous to economic activity: deforestation, irrigation, and trade plausibly modify local climate. The second is that population may be a smoothed proxy for the welfare margin that Malthusian theory actually predicts. The third is that the single-equation specification absorbs land-use composition and urbanization into the pathway fixed effect, and so cannot speak to the joint structural response in which population, land use, and urbanization may move together. We address these concerns with three triangulating exercises that take the headline regression as their reference point. Volcanic eruptions, exploited first in event-study form and then as a continuous distributed-lag regressor, provide a source of climate forcing that is exogenous to economic activity by construction. The Allen real-wage panel provides welfare-side evidence of the same Malthusian channel at city resolution. And a three-equation panel VAR uses the same exogenous volcanic forcing to identify the joint dynamics of population, cropland composition, and urbanization, recovering pathway-heterogeneous adjustment margins that the single-equation specification cannot see.

\subsection{Volcanic eruptions as natural experiments}
\label{sec:volcanic}

Major volcanic eruptions are exogenous to economic activity: they originate from tectonic forcing and inject stratospheric sulfate that cools the surface in spatially heterogeneous patterns. We first exploit five large eruptions within the ModE-RA window as a panel of cold shocks---Huaynaputina (1600), Parker (1641), Tambora (1815), Krakatoa (1883), and Pinatubo (1991)---and then move to continuous forcing from the full Sigl-Toohey ice-core record.

For each (country $i$, eruption $e$ in year $y_e$), the country's temperature shock is the post-eruption mean minus the pre-eruption baseline, $s^T_{i,e} = \tfrac13 \sum_{k=0}^{2} T_{i,y_e+k} - \tfrac15 \sum_{k=1}^{5} T_{i,y_e-k}$, with $s^P_{i,e}$ defined analogously for precipitation. The outcome is annualised log-population growth over the surrounding HYDE decade. We stack 154 countries $\times$ 5 eruptions = 719 (country, eruption) cells and run
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,e} \;=\; \alpha_e \;+\; \mu_{\tau(i)} \;+\; \beta_T \, s^T_{i,e} \;+\; \sum_\tau \theta_\tau \mathbf{1}[\tau(i){=}\tau]\, s^T_{i,e} \;+\; \beta_P \, s^P_{i,e} \;+\; \varepsilon_{i,e},
\end{equation}
with eruption fixed effects $\alpha_e$, pathway main effects $\mu_\tau$, and pathway $\times$ shock interactions $\theta_\tau$ that identify pathway-differential responses.

Across the five eruptions, the median country experienced $-0.18$ to $-0.44^\circ$C of cooling relative to its five-year baseline. The most striking individual signal is Tambora 1816 in the Netherlands: $-1.36^\circ$C against the 1421--2008 country baseline, the coldest single year in our panel, consistent with the documentary record of the ``Year Without a Summer.'' The pooled regression returns $R^2=0.34$ with eruption fixed effects absorbing most of the secular variation. The main slope on $s^T$ for the crop-dominant baseline pathway is $+0.0021$ ($p=0.10$): a 1\textdegree C cold shock lowers annual population growth by 0.21 percentage points in this group, consistent with the Malthusian prediction. The high-density intensive pathway has an interaction coefficient of $-0.0035$ ($p=0.032$), implying a near-zero net slope and a markedly more resilient demographic response to volcanic cooling (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic}). Sign and significance are robust to dropping any single eruption.

\subsubsection{Dynamic event-study: leads, lags, and parallel trends}

To trace the temporal shape of the volcanic response and to test parallel trends, we project decade-level annualised log population growth on VSSI exposure at leads and lags $h \in \{-2, -1, 0, +1, +2\}$ (decades), with country fixed effects and country-clustered standard errors. The pooled IRF (Figure~\ref{fig:sigl-irf}, panel a) shows two informative features. First, the pre-event coefficients are statistically zero: the F-test that the two lead coefficients ($h=-2$ and $h=-1$) are jointly zero returns $F = 0.33$, $p = 0.72$, comfortably passing the parallel-trends check. Second, the demographic response is \emph{lagged}: the contemporaneous coefficient is small and insignificant, while the strongest effect is at $h = +2$ decades ($\hat\beta = -36 \cdot 10^{-5}$, $p < 10^{-4}$). The two-decade lag is consistent with demographic propagation through cohort dynamics: a cold-shock-induced contraction in a HYDE decade compounds through reduced births and elevated mortality over the subsequent 10--20 years before the cumulative population effect peaks.

The per-pathway IRFs (panel b) confirm the heterogeneity recovered in the static specification, with crop-dominant systems showing a clear contemporaneous-and-lagged contraction and high-density intensive systems remaining flat across the event window.

\subsubsection{Reaching beyond 1421: Samalas, LALIA, and the pre-XV century record}
\label{sec:volcanic-pre1500}

The decade-IRF design above is bounded below by 1500 because HYDE 3.5 carries decadal population at and after 1500 but only century estimates before, and because ModE-RA does not extend prior to 1421. Both constraints are real but neither rules out a coarser pre-XV-century test, and the eVolv2k record itself reaches back to 500~BCE. We exploit two complementary sources of pre-1421 outcome variation to extend the volcanic exercise: the Allen-Studer European city wheat-price panel (1259--1500, 9 cities including London 1259, Tuscany 1264, Strasbourg 1342, Paris 1431, Antwerp 1427, and Valencia 1413) assembled from the Allen-Nuffield archive, and the Harper (2016) Roman-Egypt wheat-price series (45--650~CE) parsed from the DARMC scholarly data series. Both deliver annual price observations and so retain the temporal resolution that the century-binned HYDE intervals lose. Three findings emerge, detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500} and summarised here.

First, the same continuous-VSSI design from Section~\ref{sec:volcanic} applied to log European wheat prices 1259--1500 with city and decade fixed effects recovers a positive supply-side response peaking two years after the eruption ($\hat\beta_{L=2} = +0.0089$ per Tg of VSSI, $p=0.10$; cumulative $\sum_{L=0}^5 \hat\beta_L = +0.019$, Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-price-cont}). Applied to the 59~Tg Samalas eruption of 1257---the largest VSSI of the past 2500 years---the lag-2 coefficient implies a $\approx 52\%$ price spike by 1259, in the right neighbourhood of the documented Great Famine 1315--1317 prices and consistent with the supply-side mechanism the main text identifies.

Second, individual-event studies in 25-year bins around major eruptions reveal a strongly \emph{demand-side} response at decadal horizons. Across 9 cities, Samalas 1257 is associated with $-40\%$ log wheat prices and $+7\%$ log real wages at $h=+0$ (1257--1282, both $p<0.01$), and the 540~CE LALIA doublet (the Plague of Justinian onset) is associated with a $-94\%$ Egyptian wheat-price decline at $h=+25$ (552--587, $p<0.01$). These signs are the canonical post-plague labour-market shift: when volcanic cooling triggered famine mortality and the Black Death and Justinianic Plague compounded the contraction, surviving populations faced abundant land and labour scarcity, real wages rose, and grain prices fell. The annual supply-side spike (point one) and the decadal demand-side aftermath (point two) are mutually reinforcing pieces of the same Malthusian channel viewed at different timescales.

Third, the pathway-heterogeneous demographic test of Section~\ref{sec:volcanic} \emph{does not} survive translation to the century-binned HYDE pop intervals available before 1500: the pooled regression of annualised log-pop growth on century-summed VSSI returns $R^2 = 0.01$ and per-pathway slopes that are either small-positive or insignificant (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-pre1500}, Table~\ref{tab:volcanic-pre1500}). We treat this as honest evidence that the pre-1421 demographic record's century resolution is too coarse to resolve the pathway-heterogeneous response; the price-side annual exercises pick up the same physical mechanism through a different measurement window.

\subsection{Decomposing the Malthusian response into preventive and positive checks}
\label{sec:prevpos}

\subsubsection{Baseline: European annual panel 1751--1900}
\label{sec:prevpos-baseline}

A natural further question is whether the climate-Malthus response operates through the \emph{preventive} check (fertility postponement: cold or volatile years delay marriage and births) or the \emph{positive} check (mortality: cold or volatile years kill people). The decadal HYDE panel cannot answer this because population growth is the net of births and deaths and the difference between them is unobserved at country-decade resolution. We answer it by re-running the regression on an annual Human Mortality / Human Fertility Database panel: Sweden 1751--1899 (the canonical 149-year series), France 1820--1899, Denmark 1835--1899, Norway 1846--1899, Netherlands 1850--1899, the United Kingdom 1855--1899, plus Iceland, Italy, Switzerland, and Finland from later start years. The combined panel has 547 annual observations across ten European countries, each with age-specific mortality (infant $m_0$, child $m_5$, $m_{10}$), a fertility index, real income, and an epidemic dummy. We merge in our ModE-RA country-annual temperature and precipitation anomalies and a centred 5-year temperature standard deviation, and estimate four parallel within-country fixed-effects regressions of $\Delta \log y_{i,t}$ on $T_{it}$, $P_{it}$, and the rolling volatility, with country-clustered SEs. Adding linear country-trends does not change the coefficients.

The decomposition surfaces a clean asymmetry between the two channels. Precipitation is the mirror image of temperature on mortality (drier years carry higher mortality, $-0.030\%$ per mm of precipitation, $p < 10^{-3}$), and again is silent on fertility. This is the historically well-documented ``summer mortality'' pattern of pre-modern Europe: in the absence of refrigeration and sanitation, warm summers triggered outbreaks of dysentery and gastroenteritis that disproportionately killed infants and young children \citep{wrigley1981}. Pre-industrial European populations responded to warm years primarily by dying more, not by having fewer children. The decadal HYDE regression in Section~\ref{sec:identification} compresses this into a single coefficient that is itself sensitive to the joint structure of seasonality (Section~\ref{sec:joint-var}); the annual decomposition shows that the underlying mechanism is dominated by the positive check, with the preventive check a small and noisy secondary channel.

\subsubsection{Pre-1751 deepening via Wrigley-Schofield: future extension}
\label{sec:prevpos-ws}

The baseline sample starts at 1751 because Sweden's continuous national vital statistics begin there. Pushing the decomposition back to 1541 via the canonical Wrigley-Schofield annual aggregate England series \citep{wrigley1981} would deepen coverage of the pre-industrial Malthusian regime by 210 years. We attempted to retrieve the annual W-S aggregate crude birth and death rates from the standard public sources (Allen-Nuffield archive, Gregory Clark's UC Davis data page, Crafts-Mills replication archives, the Bank of England \emph{Millennium of Macroeconomic Data} workbook, the 2022 freely-available Schofield \emph{Parish Register Aggregate Analyses} guide). The annual aggregate vital-rate series is available only through the UK Data Service (Study Number SN 4491-1 / SN 853082), which requires academic registration and a parish-level aggregation step that we leave to a future revision. The public-domain cascade did recover the annual W-S England population series (matches the 1801 census anchor at 8.66~M); a population-only Malthusian regression on the deeper 1541--1900 sample is feasible but cannot decompose into preventive and positive checks without the rate-level series.

\subsection{Joint response of population, land use, and urbanization to climate forcing}
\label{sec:joint-var}

The headline regression treats population growth as the only endogenous outcome and absorbs land-use composition and urbanization into the pathway fixed effect. The pathway is fixed in the panel, however, while population, land use, and urbanization all change from interval to interval. The natural empirical form is a three-equation panel VAR that traces the three margins of pre-industrial structural response to climate forcing---the demographic margin, the intensive-agricultural margin (cropland-versus-grazing composition), and the unified-growth-theory exit margin (urbanization)---jointly:
\begin{align}
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln P_{i,t}      &= \alpha^P_i + \phi^P_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^P_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^P_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^P_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^P_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^P V_{it} + \varepsilon^P_{it}, \label{eq:joint-pop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln s_{i,t}      &= \alpha^C_i + \phi^C_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^C_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^C_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^C_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^C_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^C V_{it} + \varepsilon^C_{it}, \label{eq:joint-crop}\\
  \Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln u_{i,t}      &= \alpha^U_i + \phi^U_p \ln P_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_c \ln s_{i,t-1} + \phi^U_u \ln u_{i,t-1}
                                          + \gamma^U_T \bar T_{it} + \gamma^U_P \bar P_{it} + \delta^U_T \sigma^T_{it} + \beta^U V_{it} + \varepsilon^U_{it}, \label{eq:joint-urb}
\end{align}
where $s_{i,t}$ is cropland share of agricultural land (cropland $/$ (cropland $+$ grazing)), $u_{i,t}$ is urban share of total population, $V_{it}$ is the decade-summed Sigl-Toohey VSSI exposure that identifies all three equations off exogenous climate variation, and within-country FE absorbs every country-level mediator. Three findings emerge that the headline single-equation regression could not surface. First, the aggregate volcanic-forcing coefficient on population growth (Equation~\ref{eq:joint-pop}: $-0.000062$, $p<10^{-12}$) reproduces the size and sign of the headline VSSI result. The analogous coefficient on cropland-share growth (Equation~\ref{eq:joint-crop}: $-0.000006$, $p=0.40$) is precisely estimated and indistinguishable from zero, while the coefficient on urban-share growth (Equation~\ref{eq:joint-urb}: $-0.000225$, $p=0.13$) is substantively large and negative but does not clear the 10\% threshold at the aggregate level. The aggregate response to volcanic cooling is demographic in the precise statistical sense, with urbanization showing a directionally consistent but noisier slowdown that the pathway-stratified estimates will pin down.

Second, the pathway-stratified picture (Figure~\ref{fig:joint-var}) reveals a clean three-way decomposition of climate-response margins across pathways. Crop-dominant late and pastoral/mixed late pathways absorb volcanic shocks predominantly via the demographic margin: $\hat\beta = -0.000096$ ($p<10^{-6}$) and $\hat\beta = -0.000055$ ($p<10^{-5}$) on log pop growth respectively, with no significant cropland or urban response. High-density intensive systems absorb via the intensive-agricultural margin: their demographic response is indistinguishable from zero ($p=0.21$) and their urban response is null ($p=0.75$), but their cropland-share response is significantly negative ($-0.0000373$, $p=0.021$); they rebalance land use within agriculture rather than absorb the shock in people. Early extensifiers absorb via the exit margin: their demographic response is insignificant ($p=0.12$), their cropland-share response is insignificant ($p=0.19$), but their urban-share response is significantly negative ($-0.0000782$, $p=0.040$). Climate shocks slow the urbanization trajectory in countries that were already escaping the agricultural ceiling, without immediately denting the headcount.

Third, the urbanization channel is substantively meaningful even where weakly significant in the aggregate. The implied urban-share contraction for early extensifiers from a typical pre-industrial decadal VSSI exposure of $\approx 10$~Tg is $-0.078\%$ per year, or roughly $-0.78\%$ over the decade---enough to delay the urbanization trajectory by a few years per major eruption-decade and, cumulated across the 1500--1900 sample, plausibly to shift the timing of an early extensifier's structural transition by a non-trivial fraction of a century. This is the unified-growth-theory mechanism showing up in the data: volcanic cooling slows the very channel through which \citet{galor2011unified}'s escape from the Malthusian trap operates, but only in pathways far enough along that channel for the slowdown to register.

The joint specification is identified off purely exogenous variation in volcanic forcing across decades, with country fixed effects absorbing the pre-industrial pathway selection and lagged levels of $\ln P$, $\ln s$, and $\ln u$ controlling for each variable's own dynamics. We treat the three-equation result as the joint counterpart of the headline volcanic exercise rather than as a substitute. The cross-pathway asymmetry of preferred adjustment margins is itself the substantive finding: pre-industrial economies did not respond uniformly to climate shocks but absorbed them along different structural axes, and the axis depended on where the economy already was in the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory.

\section{The long shadow on modern outcomes}
\label{sec:shadow}

We have shown that the climate-induced agricultural-pathway selection that operated in the deep past still shapes how societies respond to climate shocks in the pre-industrial period, and that the response margin varies across pathways in a way that maps cleanly onto the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory. We finally ask whether the storage-demand effect itself continues to propagate into modern outcomes. We lead with the within-country evidence and then turn to the cross-country relationship and what it tells us about the institutional channel.

Using the 3{,}187-sub-unit panel and pre-industrial sub-national climate (1421--1750), we regress modern (1955--1960 versus 2015--2020) sub-national outcomes on three pre-industrial climate measures, with country fixed effects so the identifying variation is purely intra-national.

The cross-country point estimate is roughly $8\times$ the within-country one for inter-annual volatility ($-2.48$ versus $-0.31$). We read the gap not as discouraging but as informative about \emph{how} the long shadow operates. The within-country effect captures the part of the long shadow that runs through geography, directly: regions within the same country that were more climatically volatile pre-industrially reached lower carrying capacities and have less room to grow into during the modern transition. The much larger cross-country effect captures the additional part that runs through institutions, technology, and accumulated practice: climate-determined paths of agricultural and political development that, by 1900, look like country-level mediators (legal systems, kinship systems, fiscal capacity, education) and that we cannot disentangle in a panel regression. Both channels are real; the institutional channel is quantitatively larger but harder to identify cleanly, while the geographic channel is smaller but identified off pure within-country variation.

A natural worry is that modern population growth is high in tropical countries (demographic-transition lag) and pre-industrial climate volatility is high in mid-latitudes (continental seasonality): the cross-country long-shadow result could be a latitude story masquerading as a climate story. The same climate dimension that shaped which agricultural system a society adopted before 1750 leaves measurable traces in sub-national outcomes today, even after every country-level institutional confound is differenced out.

\subsection{Placebo: when does $\sigma_v^T$ have to be measured?}

If the long-shadow effect reflects genuine pre-industrial Malthusian dynamics, then $\sigma_v^T$ measured over the modern period should not predict modern population growth. We re-estimate the headline regression using $\sigma_v^T$ computed over nine alternative windows (Table~\ref{tab:placebo-window}). The window that produces the strongest predictor is the headline 1421--1750 pre-industrial window ($\hat\beta = -2.42$, $p < 10^{-21}$). The effect remains strong through 1750--1850 ($-1.58$, $p < 10^{-8}$) and 1850--1900 ($-1.48$, $p < 10^{-4}$), gradually attenuates through 1900--1950 ($-1.43$, $p < 10^{-3}$), and \emph{vanishes entirely} when $\sigma_v^T$ is computed over the modern reanalysis period 1950--2008 ($\hat\beta = -0.93$, $p = 0.16$). The progressive attenuation maps neatly onto the demographic transition: modern climate volatility is irrelevant for the demographic ceiling that pre-industrial volatility set centuries earlier.

\subsection{Other modern outcomes}

The long-shadow result on $\sigma_v^T$ is specific to modern \emph{population} outcomes. Replacing the outcome variable with Maddison/PWT GDP per capita (level 2015--2018, log growth 1995--2018) or modern $\Delta$ urban share weakens or eliminates the effect. The full-control specification on log pop growth survives ($\hat\beta = -2.48$, $p < 10^{-5}$, $R^2 = 0.37$), as does the simple specification on Maddison log GDP per capita ($+3.52$, $p < 10^{-18}$); but the GDP coefficient is entirely absorbed by absolute latitude ($\hat\beta = -0.48$, $p = 0.43$ with $|lat|$). Modern $\Delta$ urban share shows no relationship with pre-industrial $\sigma_v^T$ at any specification ($p > 0.30$). Read together with the deep-determinants robustness, this refines the long-shadow interpretation: pre-industrial climate volatility predicts the demographic trajectory specifically, not the modern level or growth of income or urbanisation.

\section{A calibrated quantitative model}
\label{sec:structural}

To organise the reduced-form coefficients into a forward-simulation framework and to compute counterfactuals, we calibrate a parsimonious pathway-specific dynamic model. The model takes the empirical pathway-stratified coefficients of Section~\ref{sec:stage2} as structural inputs rather than estimating deep production-function parameters that the data do not identify. We accordingly call this a \emph{calibrated quantitative model} rather than a structural estimation.

\subsection{Model}

For country $i$ in pathway $\tau$ and HYDE interval $[t, t+1)$, annualised log population growth follows
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{ann}} \ln N_{it} = \alpha_\tau + \beta_\tau(t)\, \tilde d_{it} + \gamma_\tau\, \tilde T_{it} + \delta_\tau\, \tilde\sigma^T_{it} + \varepsilon_{it},
\label{eq:struct}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde d, \tilde T, \tilde\sigma^T$ are within-country deviations from the pre-industrial country mean, and the Malthusian density coefficient is permitted to evolve over time:
$\beta_\tau(t) = \beta_{0,\tau} + \eta_\tau \cdot (t - 1421)/100$. The slope $\eta_\tau$ captures the rate at which the Malthusian feedback weakens with the secular accumulation of human capital, literacy, and storage---calibrated from how fast $\hat\beta_\tau$ moves toward zero across our three subperiods (Table~\ref{tab:malthus-subperiod}). All other pathway-specific parameters are taken from the pre-industrial Malthus regression (Section~\ref{sec:stage2}, Table~\ref{tab:malthus}).

\subsection{Validation}

The model captures within-country growth deviations modestly: pseudo-$R^2$ in one-step-ahead predictions ranges from $0.26$ (early extensifiers) to $-0.18$ (high-density intensive). The model does \emph{not} reproduce the cross-country long-shadow result quantitatively: simulating terminal cumulative growth and regressing on pre-industrial $\sigma_v^T$ returns a non-significant coefficient ($\hat\beta_{\sigma_v}^{\text{sim}}=+0.18$, $p=0.56$). We read this failure as informative rather than fatal: the cross-country long shadow operates primarily through institutional and technological mediators that the parsimonious within-country Malthusian dynamics do not capture, consistent with the institutional-channel interpretation we already gave in Section~\ref{sec:shadow}.

\subsection{Counterfactual experiments}

The model's value is for counterfactual computations that the empirical regressions cannot perform directly. We run three.

\paragraph{CF1: pathway reassignment.} Re-simulate each pastoral/mixed-late country under the calibrated parameters of the high-density-intensive pathway, keeping its actual climate trajectory. Median cumulative pre-industrial growth lift: $+0.197$ log-units, equivalent to a $+21.8\%$ population gain at the end of the pre-industrial period ($N=65$ pastoral countries; IQR $+7.1\%$ to $+33.0\%$). The intensive-pathway parameters convert the same climate inputs into substantially faster demographic accumulation, dominated by the stronger anti-Malthusian density coefficient and the smaller volatility drag $\delta_T$.

\paragraph{CF2: no volcanism, 1500--1900.} Identify the 11 eruption years 1500--1900 with stratospheric sulfur injection $\geq 5$ Tg in the Sigl-Toohey eVolv2k record, and set the within-interval $\sigma^T$ to zero whenever a HYDE interval contains such a year. Cumulative population effect across the four pathways:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=2em]
  \item Crop-dominant late:      $+23.2\%$ population gain ($N=45$ countries)
  \item Pastoral/mixed late:     $-15.1\%$ ($N=65$)
  \item High-density intensive:  $+29.1\%$ ($N=16$)
  \item Early extensifiers:      $+22.7\%$ ($N=27$)
\end{itemize}
Three of the four pathways gain from removing volcanism, as expected from negative $\delta_{T,\tau}$. Pastoral systems \emph{lose} from removing volcanism: their calibrated $\delta_T$ is positive (mobility hedges volatility favourably). The sign asymmetry across pathways is what the model exists to quantify; it would not have been recoverable from the reduced-form regressions alone.

\paragraph{CF3: progressive climate decoupling.} Shrink within-interval $\sigma^T$ by a fixed weight $w \in \{1.0, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.0\}$ for all countries and recompute cumulative pre-industrial growth. Figure~\ref{fig:cf} shows the mean cumulative growth deviation across $w$ per pathway. The intensive pathway benefits monotonically from decoupling (up to $+0.55$ log-units under full decoupling); the crop-dominant pathway shows a small peak around $w=0.25$; pastoral and early extensifiers show non-monotonic patterns reflecting their sign-flipping calibrated $\delta_T$.

\subsection{Welfare counterfactuals via Allen wages}

The counterfactuals above translate exogenous shocks into population effects. To translate population into welfare, we use the Allen real-wage Malthus regression coefficients from Section~\ref{sec:allen}: $\hat\beta_{\ln \text{pop}} = -0.285$ on lagged log population, with country fixed effects, on the 1421--1850 sample of six European countries. The interpretation is the canonical Malthusian one: holding everything else constant, a 10\% rise in lagged population reduces real wages by 2.9\%. Per-capita welfare and total (utilitarian) welfare in each counterfactual are
\begin{equation}
\Delta \ln w_{\tau}^{\text{per cap}} = \hat\beta_{\ln \text{pop}} \cdot \Delta \ln N_\tau, \qquad
\Delta W_{\tau}^{\text{total}} = \Delta \ln N_\tau + \Delta \ln w_\tau^{\text{per cap}}.
\end{equation}

Three substantive points emerge. First, the canonical Malthusian welfare paradox: removing volcanic forcing increases the headcount but reduces per-capita real wages. For high-density intensive systems, removing 1500--1900 volcanism produces $+29\%$ more people each $-6.9\%$ poorer on average---a net utilitarian gain of $+0.18$ log-welfare but a clearly worse outcome for the representative individual in the affected centuries. Second, the pastoral exception is informative: removing volcanism reduces population in pastoral systems (their $\delta_T$ is positive, so volatility helps their growth) and therefore \emph{raises} per-capita wages, but the total welfare effect is negative because there are fewer people. Third, the magnitudes are economically meaningful: a $5$--$12\%$ per-capita wage shift over 400 years is substantively large relative to documented pre-industrial wage trajectories \citep{voigtlander2013how, clark2007farewell}, and the $+14$--$32\%$ total welfare ranges across counterfactuals indicate that pathway-climate interactions are first-order for pre-industrial development outcomes.

\subsection{Reading the model honestly}

The exercise is not a deep structural estimation. It takes pathway-specific reduced-form coefficients and runs them forward to produce counterfactual magnitudes. It would not be appropriate as a basis for welfare claims that depend on deep production-function elasticities or intertemporal-substitution parameters. Two pieces of information come out cleanly: (i) the order of magnitude of the demographic effect of agricultural-pathway choice (about $\pm 20$--$30\%$ population over the pre-industrial period for plausibly-comparable countries), and (ii) the order of magnitude of cumulative volcanic forcing across 400 years ($\pm 15$--$29\%$ population by pathway, with associated wage and welfare shifts in Table~\ref{tab:welfare}). These are quantitative anchors the reduced-form coefficients alone do not deliver. The model treats population as the single endogenous outcome and is silent on the joint cropland-and-urbanization dynamics that Section~\ref{sec:joint-var} surfaces; an extension to a three-equation forward simulation is a natural next step, but here we keep the structural exercise parsimonious enough to interpret one moment at a time.

\section{Critical robustness and limitations}
\label{sec:robustness}

Several robustness concerns warrant explicit treatment. We organise them in roughly decreasing order of importance for the headline claims.

The new climate panel calibrates well against the modern instrumental record. Section~\ref{sec:data} reported median country-month bias of $0.61^\circ$C, median country-monthly correlation of $0.971$, and median annual anomaly correlation of $0.938$ between ModE-RA $+$ CRU climatology and our country-level ERA5 rebuild. An earlier draft of this paper reported much worse numbers because it mistakenly compared country-level ModE-RA against a 25-region ERA5 panel and confused a resolution mismatch for a model deficit; this version compares like with like. The full picture of merged-panel uncertainty across the entire 1421--2025 window is in Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble}. We aggregate ModE-RA's published ensemble $1\sigma$ fields to country-month resolution by the same area-weighting procedure used for the levels, and report them alongside the ERA5--ModE-RA cross-product disagreement in the 1950--2008 overlap and a literature-based ERA5 instrumental floor for the post-2008 period. Temperature uncertainty falls monotonically from $0.90^\circ$C in 1421--1500 (sparse proxy assimilation) to $0.79^\circ$C in 1500--1700, $0.64^\circ$C in 1700--1850, and $0.37^\circ$C in 1850--1949; precipitation uncertainty traces the same secular decline from $31.6$ to $15.4$~mm/month. The 1950--2008 overlap returns an ERA5--ModE-RA monthly RMSE of about $1.0^\circ$C in temperature and $33$--$44$~mm/month in precipitation, both larger than either dataset's intrinsic $1\sigma$ and informative about cross-product disagreement that neither ensemble alone captures. Post-2008 the merged panel is ERA5-only at a much tighter $\approx 0.1^\circ$C and $\approx 5$~mm/month instrumental floor. The effect sizes of every headline finding exceed the local 1$\sigma$ noise floor by factors of 2--4 across the relevant eras, so the substantive conclusions are not fragile to ensemble-member draw or to the choice of which product is used in the overlap.

The panel also passes documentary cross-validation. We checked five well-documented historical climate-driven famines---the Great Frost of 1709 in Europe, the Year Without Summer in 1816 after Tambora, the Irish Potato Famine 1845--49, the North China Famine 1876--79, and the Bengal Famine 1943---and ModE-RA cleanly reproduces the climate anomaly in the affected countries' annual temperature series. HYDE's decadal-resolution population data, however, smooths over short-lived demographic crises: only the Irish Famine produces a statistically meaningful decadal change in HYDE population, while Bengal 1943 and the Year Without Summer 1816 are too brief to register in HYDE's coarse temporal grid. This is a limitation of HYDE, not of the new climate panel, and it motivates eventual extension to higher-frequency historical demographic data of the kind \citet{wrigley1981} compiled for England. Where dense instrumental records exist (Europe from $\sim$1700, North America from $\sim$1850) the reanalysis is constrained tightly; in data-sparse regions (much of sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and South America pre-1850) monthly variation reflects the model prior to a greater degree, and we have therefore avoided claims that depend on year-to-year detail in data-sparse cells. The productive-months index is averaged over 330 years and the Malthusian regression uses interval-mean climate, both of which are robust to monthly-scale noise.

The pathway typology that organises the empirical work is defensible on standard clustering diagnostics. Silhouette scores across $K \in \{2,\ldots,8\}$ peak at $K{=}5$ (0.399), with a sharp drop at $K{=}6$ (0.227); bootstrap stability of the partition over 200 resamples gives median co-cluster probability $0.84$, with the pastoral cluster the most stable (mean $0.88$) and the early-extensifier cluster the least (mean $0.60$, reflecting genuine overlap with the crop-dominant pathway). Adjusted Rand Index against re-clusterings at $K=6$ is $0.53$, consistent with $K=5$ being a coarsening of a fine partition rather than an arbitrary cut. The full diagnostics are in Figure~\ref{fig:pathway-validation}. One conceptual concern remains: the five pathways are estimated from HYDE trajectory features that include 1750-era density and crop share, so pathway assignment is partly a downstream outcome of the agricultural choices we are trying to explain at the country level. The sub-national specification in Section~\ref{sec:subnational} sidesteps this concern by identifying the storage-demand effect off within-country variation, and readers should interpret the country-level Stage 1 results as descriptive and the sub-national ones as causal. The HYDE Manski-bound interval is essentially zero-width on our headline coefficients: HYDE publishes three uncertainty scenarios that are uniform $0.80$ and $1.20$ scalings of the baseline values, so our outcomes (crop share, a ratio of land-use levels; log density, a difference of logs) are scale-invariant by construction. The within-country slope on productive months at 1750 ranges over $[+0.0669, +0.0669]$ for crop share and $[+0.6567, +0.6576]$ for log density across scenarios---not a partial-identification triumph but evidence that the headline coefficients are not driven by HYDE's own level uncertainty.

A standard objection to cross-country long-run regressions is that the predictor proxies for a deep determinant of development---distance from a Neolithic origin centre \citep{putterman2008}, ancestry-adjusted genetic diversity \citep{ashraf2013out}, state antiquity, landlocked-ness, or terrain ruggedness---rather than the channel the paper claims. We add the standard battery: haversine distance to the nearest of seven Neolithic origin centres (Fertile Crescent, Yangtze and Yellow River China, Sahel/West Africa, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and Eastern North America), migratory distance from Addis Ababa, a landlocked dummy, log land area, a centroid-based ruggedness proxy, mean pre-industrial temperature, and absolute latitude. Added sequentially, they leave the $\sigma_v^T \to \log$ pop growth coefficient remarkably stable (Table~\ref{tab:deep}). Bockstette-Chanda-Putterman state history and the Olsson-Hibbs biogeographic conditions index would be natural additional controls; their tables are published with the respective replication packages and would need to be hand-coded for the 196 HYDE countries. We leave them as planned-on-request extensions.

Two complementary checks confirm the volatility long-shadow is not a deep-determinants artifact. Distance to Neolithic centres does itself distinguish pathways in a one-way ANOVA ($F=4.96$, $p=0.003$), but productive-months is the stronger Stage 1 predictor ($F=6.31$, $p<10^{-3}$); in a joint multinomial logit, productive-months retains independent significance ($p=0.001$ in the crop-dominant equation) after controlling for Neolithic distance. Interacting Sigl-Toohey VSSI with standardised log distance to Neolithic centres in the volcanic regression returns an interaction coefficient of $-3.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ with $p=0.57$: the pathway-heterogeneous volcanic response is not driven by countries' deep-historical exposure to agriculture.

The productive-months index is a custom measure with specific thresholds, and a referee will ask whether it survives substitution by a published agro-ecological classification. The FAO Global Agro-Ecological Zones (GAEZ) database is the standard such layer; we replicate its rain-fed-wheat suitability logic from our own monthly climate panel rather than downloading the full FAO grid. The wheat-suitability index is the geometric mean of four trapezoidal membership functions on growing degree days base 5\textdegree C (1500--3500 ideal), annual precipitation (250--1500~mm ideal), frost months ($\le 5$), and warmest-month mean temperature (18--30\textdegree C). The resulting index also distinguishes pathways (ANOVA $F = 3.79$, $p = 0.012$), with the highest values in the high-density intensive pathway (mean $0.53$) and the lowest in pastoral/mixed ($0.20$). A multinomial logit on wheat suitability returns pseudo-$R^2 = 0.026$ and LLR $p = 0.017$. The discriminating power is weaker than the productive-months index ($F=6.31$, $p<10^{-3}$) because wheat suitability is a narrower agronomic criterion than rain-fed cropping in general, but the qualitative ranking of pathways is preserved.

A related question is whether the default area-weighted climate aggregation hides results that would change under economically meaningful weighting. The default treats Siberia, the Sahara, and the Australian outback as fully informative about Russian, Egyptian, and Australian climate respectively. We re-aggregate ModE-RA twice more: once using HYDE 3.5 gridded population (mean of 1500--1700 timesteps as a pre-industrial reference) as the cell weight, and once using HYDE gridded cropland-plus-half-grazing as the cell weight. The second specification is the most aggressive: cells with no pre-industrial agricultural land get zero weight, so a country like Egypt is aggregated effectively over the Nile valley alone. Headline results are robust to all three weightings. The Stage~1 ANOVA on productive months is $F=6.305$ (area), $6.307$ (pop), and $6.315$ (cropland), with $p\approx 0.0005$ in each case. The sub-national within-country slope on crop share is $+0.0669$ ($p=0.019$) area-weighted and $+0.0658$ ($p=0.023$) pop-weighted. The long-shadow $\sigma_v^T \to \log$ pop growth slope with absolute-latitude controls is $-1.75$ ($p=0.003$) area-weighted, $-1.88$ ($p=0.002$) pop-weighted, and $-1.86$ ($p=0.002$) cropland-weighted. Egypt's productive-months index is $0.00$ under all three weightings (Egypt's rainfall is below the rain-fed threshold everywhere in the country), confirming that its irrigation-pioneer status is robust to the aggregation method. The largest individual-country shifts under pop-weighting occur in continental countries where population concentrates in a sub-region (Canada's pre-industrial $\sigma_s^T$ drops by $0.64$\textdegree C, Kuwait's by $0.41$\textdegree C, Libya's by $0.31$\textdegree C), but none of the substantive conclusions change. We retain the area-weighted panel as the headline because it is the more standard choice in climate-economy work, with the pop-weighted and cropland-weighted panels available in the replication package.

A final concern is the identification weight to place on individual versus pooled volcanic events. Section~\ref{sec:volcanic} reported a placebo on the pooled five-eruption test; we also test the cleanest single event, Tambora 1815, against 1{,}000 single-year placebos drawn from 1755--1894. The Tambora-alone pathway-shock interaction is statistically indistinguishable from zero ($\hat\beta = -0.0031$, $p=0.68$), and the real coefficient sits at the 65th percentile of the placebo distribution. Individual eruptions do not separately identify the pathway-differential demographic response; the pooled five-eruption result is genuine but rests on across-event efficiency, not on any single event being decisive. We therefore weight the discrete-event volcanic study as suggestive triangulation and rest the volcanic identification on the continuous Sigl-Toohey forcing exercise instead.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The structure of climate---specifically, the share of months that can support rain-fed cropping---selected agricultural systems in the deep past, and that selection still shapes how societies absorb climate shocks today. The new paleo-economic panel we assemble makes this empirical claim addressable for the first time at country-monthly resolution and back to the early fifteenth century, and the within-country specification we run on it returns seven mutually reinforcing pieces of evidence. Storage demand, not raw temperature seasonality, governs pathway assignment, and the effect is identified off purely within-country variation at $3.5\times$ the between-country estimate. The 1421--1950 Malthusian regression recovers pathway-heterogeneous coefficients and a within-paper signature of the demographic transition, with the volatility coefficient flipping sign as twentieth-century monoculture replaces mixed pre-industrial systems. Continuous volcanic forcing from Sigl-Toohey 1500--1900 produces a sharply pathway-heterogeneous demographic response, and the pre-XV-century extension to the Samalas 1257 eruption corroborates the channel on annual wheat prices and survives both conflict-and-pandemic controls and region-aware exposure weighting. The joint three-equation VAR for population, cropland composition, and urbanization reveals the cleanest pathway asymmetry of the paper: traditional crop and pastoral systems absorb climate shocks demographically, intensive systems rebalance land-use composition without losing people, and early-extensifier systems absorb by slowing urbanization---each pathway adjusting along a different structural axis, and the axis depending on where the economy already was in the unified-growth-theory escape trajectory. An annual European panel of age-specific mortality and fertility 1751--1900 then decomposes the Malthusian response into its preventive (fertility) and positive (mortality) checks: the response is dominated by the positive check on infant and child mortality, the historically documented summer-mortality pattern that the decadal HYDE regression compresses into a single coefficient. The Allen real-wage panel returns the canonical Malthusian price-on-population coefficient at city resolution. And pre-industrial climate volatility 1421--1750 remains the single strongest cross-country predictor of modern population growth, surviving latitude and an extended deep-determinant battery.

The conceptual frame is not original to us. \citet{galor2011unified} provides unified growth theory; \citet{matranga2024} provides the climate-drives-agriculture mechanism we extend. Our contribution is empirical and methodological. We bring real annual climate forcing to the pre-industrial period for the first time at country-monthly resolution; we identify off within-country variation where the data permit; we use exogenous volcanic forcing to identify a joint structural-response system in which population, land use, and urbanization respond along different axes by pathway; and we show that the same climate dimension that determined which agricultural system a society adopted also determined how quickly that society escaped the Malthusian trap. The paleo-economic dataset that makes the exercise possible is, we think, the more durable contribution. Questions about pre-industrial development that the existing literature has had to leave to plausible storytelling can now be addressed econometrically.

A more careful version of the Matranga story---one in which the relevant climate measure is the joint structure of temperature and precipitation that determines storage demand, not raw temperature seasonality---survives encounter with country-level monthly climate. Seasonality, properly measured, casts a long shadow.

\section{A two-stage framework}
\label{app:theory}

We sketch a semi-formal two-stage model in the spirit of \citet{ashraf2011dynamics}: it delivers the testable predictions used in the body of the paper without insisting on full general-equilibrium proofs.

\subsection*{Stage 1: climate and agricultural system selection}

A representative household with mean-variance utility $u(c) = c - \tfrac{\rho}{2}\mathrm{Var}(c)$ chooses agricultural technology $\tau \in \{I, P, X\}$ ($I$ = intensive crop, $P$ = extensive pastoral, $X$ = irrigation-based) given the climate endowment $(\Pi, \sigma_v)$, where $\Pi \in [0,12]$ is the share of climatically productive months and $\sigma_v$ is inter-annual volatility. Period consumption under each technology is
\begin{equation}
  c_I \;=\; A_I \, \Pi \;-\; \kappa_I \;+\; \lambda_I \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
  \quad
  c_P \;=\; A_P \, m \;+\; \lambda_P \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
  \quad
  c_X \;=\; A_X \;-\; \kappa_X \;+\; \lambda_X \sigma_v \epsilon_t,
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon_t$ unit-variance, $A_\tau$ the deterministic productivity of each technology, $\kappa_\tau$ the fixed cost of adoption (granaries, irrigation works; no fixed cost for pastoral), $m \in (0,1)$ the mobility-based recovery of annual surplus available to pastoralists, and $\lambda_\tau$ the exposure of $\tau$ to climate volatility. The intensive technology delivers output proportional to $\Pi$ because rain-fed cropping cannot operate during non-productive months; the pastoral technology operates year-round through mobility but at lower average productivity; irrigation bypasses $\Pi$ at the cost of $\kappa_X$.

Expected utility under $\tau$ is $V(\tau) = E[c_\tau] - \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_\tau^2 \sigma_v^2$. The selection condition $\tau^\ast = I$ requires $V(I) > V(P)$ and $V(I) > V(X)$:
\begin{equation}
  A_I \, \Pi \;-\; \kappa_I \;-\; \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_I^2 \sigma_v^2
  \;>\;
  A_P \, m \;-\; \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lambda_P^2 \sigma_v^2.
  \label{eq:I-vs-P}
\end{equation}
Solving for $\Pi$ gives the productive-months threshold above which intensive crop dominates pastoral:
\begin{equation}
  \Pi \;>\; \Pi^*(\sigma_v) \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{A_I}\!\left[A_P m + \kappa_I + \tfrac{\rho}{2}(\lambda_I^2 - \lambda_P^2)\sigma_v^2\right].
  \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
Analogous reasoning against $X$ yields a much higher threshold (or, equivalently, $X$ dominates only when $\Pi$ is small \emph{and} $A_X$ is large enough to offset $\kappa_X$), which accounts for the rarity of pure irrigation pioneers in our data (Egypt is the only one). Comparative statics are immediate. The threshold $\Pi^*$ is increasing in pastoral mobility benefit $m$ and intensive fixed cost $\kappa_I$, decreasing in intensive productivity $A_I$. With $\lambda_P^2 > \lambda_I^2$ (intensive systems' storage and granaries hedge volatility better than pastoral mobility), $\Pi^*$ is decreasing in volatility $\sigma_v$: when climate is more volatile, intensive crop dominates at lower productive-month counts, because the storage hedge is more valuable. Stage 1 thus predicts that the share of climatically productive months predicts agricultural pathway assignment; formally, $\Pr(\tau^\ast = I \mid \Pi, \sigma_v) = \Phi\bigl((\Pi - \Pi^*(\sigma_v))/\eta\bigr)$ for unobserved heterogeneity scale $\eta$ and Gaussian CDF $\Phi$.

\subsection*{Stage 2: pathway, Malthusian feedback, and escape}

Given the selected system $\tau^\ast$, population dynamics follow
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \ln P_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i \;+\; \beta(\tau^\ast)\, d_{i,t-1} \;+\; \gamma(\tau^\ast)\, T_{it} \;+\; \delta(\tau^\ast)\,\sigma_v^T(T_{it}) \;+\; \eta(\tau^\ast)\, h_{it} \;+\; \varepsilon_{it},
  \label{eq:malthus}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is log population density, $T_{it}$ is contemporaneous mean temperature, $\sigma_v^T$ is its inter-annual standard deviation, and $h_{it}$ is a technology or human-capital proxy. The pathway-specific parameters capture three margins. The Malthusian density coefficient $\beta(\tau^\ast)$ is predicted to be stronger in pastoral than intensive systems ($|\beta(P)| > |\beta(I)|$): pastoral systems sit closer to a tight Malthusian ceiling because they cannot store, while intensive systems carry capital that smooths the feedback. The contemporaneous climate responses $\gamma(\tau^\ast)$ and $\delta(\tau^\ast)$ go in opposite directions across pathways: pastoralists' mobility hedges level shocks but their thinner safety net amplifies volatility shocks ($|\delta(P)| > |\delta(I)|$), while intensive systems suffer level shocks (killing frosts) more but absorb volatility better through storage. The rate at which human capital weakens the Malthusian constraint, $\eta(\tau^\ast)$, is faster in intensive systems---literacy, urbanisation, and capital accumulation accelerate there---so escape is earlier on the intensive arm. Equation~\ref{eq:malthus} is the appendix counterpart of equation~\eqref{eq:malthus-main} in the main text; the empirical analogue with interval-mean climate is equation~\eqref{eq:malthus2}, estimated in Section~\ref{sec:identification}.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The pre-1500 record carries a clean and direct \emph{annual} supply-side signal of volcanic cooling on grain prices (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-price-cont}): the peak response is at lag two years, the standard agricultural transmission lag, and the magnitude implied for Samalas is consistent with the Great Famine. The same record carries a \emph{decadal} demand-side signal of volcanic cooling on grain prices and real wages (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-price-event}, Table~\ref{tab:volcanic-pre1500}) of the Black-Death-bonus form. The two signals are not in tension: the annual price spike comes from supply, the decadal price decline and wage rise come from the demographic contraction the spike triggered. Both are Malthusian. The pathway-heterogeneous demographic regression (Figure~\ref{fig:volcanic-pre1500}) cannot be run at century resolution and we say so; the price-side exercises preserve the temporal resolution that the demographic exercise loses.

\subsection*{C.1 Conflict and pandemic robustness}
\label{app:conflict-robust}

The most obvious confound for any pre-1500 climate-cum-price exercise is that volcanic eruptions sit inside dense and overlapping histories of war, siege, and plague: Samalas~1257 inside the Mongol invasions of Eastern Europe, the 1453 Kuwae eruption inside the fall of Constantinople and the end of the Hundred Years' War, the 540~CE LALIA doublet inside the Plague of Justinian and the Gothic Wars, and Cyprian~249 inside the Crisis of the Third Century. The price-side exercises above could in principle pick up these contemporaneous shocks rather than volcanic cooling. We address the concern by adding a city-year conflict-and-pandemic regressor layer to the continuous-VSSI distributed-lag regression and the Samalas event study, and by first verifying that the regressors retain enough within-city, within-decade variation to be identified at all in a two-way FE specification.

The Samalas event-study point estimate at $h=+0$ moves from $-0.262$ to $-0.261$. Volcanic forcing is not a war-or-plague confound. The controls themselves carry interpretable and well-identified coefficients: each additional active war in a city's region raises log wheat price by 4.6\% ($p < 10^{-3}$); plague years are associated with 6.8\% higher contemporaneous prices ($p = 0.04$, the year-of-arrival market panic before the demand crash dominates the multi-year aftermath that city + decade FE absorb); city-year siege observations carry $-23\%$ prices ($p < 10^{-3}$, consistent with siege economics in which the besieged city's grain stock is impounded and the broader market crashes when supply lines reopen).

\paragraph{Limitations.} The Egyptian price subset is a single-city panel of 68 annual observations; after decade fixed effects, conflict and pandemic indicators retain almost no residual variation and we cannot run the corresponding robustness check inside that panel. The European pre-1500 price panel is the right place to test the volcanic claim against the conflict confound, and it survives. We treat the Egyptian results in Table~\ref{tab:volcanic-pre1500} as suggestive rather than primary, and rest the appendix's identification weight on the multi-city European panel.

\subsection*{C.2 Region-aware VSSI exposure}
\label{app:regional-vssi}

The continuous-VSSI design in §\ref{sec:volcanic-pre1500} pools all eVolv2k eruptions regardless of latitude into a single annual forcing series, while the actual climate response is hemispherically asymmetric: tropical eruptions cool both hemispheres, NH extra-tropical eruptions cool the NH almost exclusively, and SH extra-tropical eruptions cool the SH \citep{toohey2017}. All Allen panel cities sit between 32$^\circ$N and 58$^\circ$N, so they should respond to tropical and NH-extra-tropical forcing and barely to SH-only events. Pooling SH-only VSSI into their regressor is measurement error that biases the estimate toward zero. We address this with two refinements: a latitude-band specification that drops SH-only contribution from each NH city's exposure (a 20\% leakage factor is allowed), and a distance-weighted specification that further attenuates each eruption's contribution to each city by a Gaussian in latitudinal distance with a 30$^\circ$ bandwidth.

\paragraph{Reading the table.} Three results. First, latitude-band trimming (column 2) barely moves the global estimate; pre-1500 SH-only VSSI is too small a share of total mass to matter as measurement error. Second, distance weighting (column 3) tightens the estimate sharply: lag-2 jumps from $+0.0091$ ($p=0.10$) to $+0.0294$ ($p=0.014$), lags 3 and 4 reach $p<0.05$ and $p<0.01$, and the cumulative 5-year response is $+0.1024$ ($p=0.005$). Third, the distance-weighted coefficient scales with the smaller regressor (Samalas 1257 at $-8.4^{\circ}$ enters London at $51.5^{\circ}$ with effective weight $\approx 0.14$, so the effective Samalas exposure for London is $\approx 8.1$~Tg, not 59~Tg); applying the lag-2 coefficient gives a $+24\%$ price spike at 1259 from lag-2 alone and a cumulative $+83\%$ over five years for London, with Tuscany ($43.8^{\circ}$N) experiencing a larger response by virtue of its smaller latitudinal distance to the Samalas vent. \paragraph{Interpretation.} The distance-weighted specification captures cross-city variation in the volcanic-cooling channel that the global pool averages out: cities at lower latitudes feel tropical eruptions more, and cities closer to NH extra-tropical vents (Iceland for the Eldgj\'a 939 and 1108 unidentified-NH events) feel those more. Adding this physically motivated weighting recovers a sharp lag-2--4 wheat-price response consistent with the canonical agricultural-transmission interpretation, with magnitudes that match the historical record. We treat the distance-weighted spec as the preferred pre-1500 specification and the global spec as a conservative lower bound on the volcanic signal.

\subsection*{C.3 Medieval English mortality, 1300--1500: a future extension}
\label{app:medieval-mortality}

The pre-XV-century volcanic appendix (\S\ref{app:volcanic-pre1500}) extends the volcanic exercise back to the 1257 Samalas eruption using city-level grain prices, but stops short of a direct mortality-side test for medieval England. The natural complement is a manor-level mortality event-study using the Hatcher-Bailey reconstructions from estates such as Halesowen, Westminster, and Winchester, run against the eVolv2k volcanic record and the OWDA / PAGES~2k climate reconstructions, with the canonical Black Death wave windows entering as explicit controls (1347--53, 1361--63, 1374--75, 1399--1402).

We attempted to retrieve a continuous manor-level mortality series through the standard public channels: the \citet{voigtlander2013how} \emph{Review of Economic Studies} replication archive (which redistributes the Bairoch city-population dataset but not manorial mortality), the Campbell (2016) \emph{Great Transition} Cambridge UP supplementary materials (publicly available materials do not include the manorial series), the \citet{hatcher1986} \emph{Economic History Review} article (single-site Christ Church Canterbury data 1395--1505, with no Black Death anchor), and the Page (2003) Winchester pipe-roll archive (UK Data Service Study SN 4086, registration-gated). None of the public sources yields a continuous manor-level series of sufficient length and coverage to run the event study cleanly.

What the published demographic literature documents at point-estimate resolution is consistent with the macro-pattern this paper builds. Manorial Black Death mortality 1348--49 averaged 40--45\% on English estates (Halesowen, Westminster, Winchester among the highest), with secondary peaks at 1361 (10--20\%) and 1374--75 (5--10\%) \citep{hatcherbailey2001}. National mortality estimates are similar in magnitude---\citet{hatcher1977} puts the 1348 mortality at 30--45\% with high regional variance, and \citet{benedictow2004} compiles a survey-of-surveys consistent with that range. Two of these mortality peaks coincide directly with major volcanic eruption decades: the Samalas 1257 eruption (eVolv2k VSSI 59~Tg, the largest of the past 2{,}500 years) precedes the Great Famine 1315--17 and the Black Death 1347--53 by 30--40 and 90--100 years respectively, while the 1345 eruption (15~Tg) sits inside the immediate pre-Black-Death decade, the cold and famine years widely held to have weakened the European disease environment ahead of the pandemic onset. The continuous-series event-study at manor resolution is left as a future extension, conditional on UK Data Service access to Study SN 4086 plus its peer reconstructions.
